1. GENERAL INFORMATION 
 
Chuya village is located right at the edge of the mountains, about 30 km from Nurota district 
center. The population is 3740, of which 1820 females and 1920 males. The number of 
youths under 18 is 1062. The winter temperature can drop to – 40 C for a short period 
(usually the winter temperature is about -10 to – 15 C). There is an acute shortage of water 
deficiency in Chuya village. Therefore, water is drawn through an artisan water well at a 150 
m depth. The water from the well is salty in most cases and is used for irrigation and animal 
consumption. Yet, it is hardly sufficient for the needs of the local community. The settlement 
is attached to vast forest land falling in the cold winter desert (Figure 1). 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
1.1. Project site in the cold winter desert in Chuya 
The cold winter desert where the project activities were undertaken falls within the vast forest 
region. The site selected for the project activity is primarily uncultivated forest land. Some 
activities are going around the site, particularly the planting of pistachios (Figure 2). The 
selected site has no source of irrigation water and crop production is dependent on rainfall 
which is around 150 mm annually and distributed over late autumn, winter and spring 
months.  The soil is filled with gravel with varying levels of degradation. Natural vegetation 
on the site represents a number of desert weeds and grasses (Figure 2).  
1.2. Integrated natural resource management 
Due to limited rainfall and degraded soil, chickpea was identified as a suitable crop that can 
be grown under prevalent rainfall, and on partially degraded soil. Chickpeas are traditionally 
planted in spring because farmers consider that harsh winter conditions will be detrimental to 
the crops planted in autumn. However, the spring-planted chickpea crop can’t utilize the 
rainfall occurring during late autumn and winter. Also, there is a new chickpea variety that 
can survive winter temperatures in the neighboring Karshi province. 
Chickpea cultivation can also help improve the health of degraded forest soils. Besides, 
organic manure and compost can be prepared from desert vegetation and applied to the soil. 
Being a leguminous crop, chickpea needs much lower nitrogenous fertilizers compared to

1.3. Integrated crop management 
Integrated crop management refers to the production system which conserves and enhances 
natural resources while producing food on an economically viable and sustainable basis. It 
takes into consideration the interactions between crop biology, the growing environment, and 
land management practices. It is particularly appropriate for small farmers because it aims to 
minimize dependence on purchased inputs and to make the fullest possible use of locally 
available natural resources, indigenous technical knowledge, and land-use practices.

2 CHICKPEA PRODUCTION 
2.1.  Introduction 
Chickpea, locally called “Noxut” is an important food legume embedded into numerous 
Uzbek cuisines. Chickpeas are cultivated in all provinces of Uzbekistan in small or large 
areas. This is considered a secondary food crop in Uzbekistan. There is no nationally 
organized research and extension work on chickpea cultivation in Uzbekistan.  Therefore, 
information on its cultivation, seed production, and rural advisory services is lacking. Being a 
drought-tolerant crop, chickpea is primarily cultivated on rainfed land in Uzbekistan. 
Chickpeas are traditionally planted in early spring, but recent research findings show that 
cold-tolerant chickpea varieties can be planted during autumn. The autumn-planted chickpea 
produces a higher yield than spring-planted. 
2.2.  Chickpea plant 
Chickpea, botanically called Cicer arietinum is an annual plant belonging to the Fabaceae 
family. The plants are herbaceous, with annual growth habits. Primary branches appear on the 
main stem from the base. The plant architecture forms a small bush with dispersed, spreading 
branches. The plant surface is covered with glandular or non-glandular hair, but there are 
exceptions. The plant height depends on growing conditions, which can often reach up to 100 
cm, and occasionally reach 150 cm. Based on seed size and color, there are two types of 
cultivated chickpeas, desi and kabuli.   Desi type chickpeas have generally smaller (around 
0.2 g per seed) seeds than kabuli (around 4 g per seed). The colors of desi chickpeas include 
cream, yellow, brown, black and green. The Kabuli chickpea has mostly cream color and is 
preferred in Central Asia.

2.3.  Climatic requirements 
Chickpea is a cool-seasoned crop but adapted to a range of temperatures. In Central Asia, cold-
tolerant chickpea varieties can be planted in autumn if winter temperatures don’t fall below 
−10⁰C but remain above −20⁰C for more than a few days. Under severe winter conditions, 
chickpeas are planted in early spring. Chickpea yields are high when the temperatures during 
daytime and night are 70 to 80F and 64 to 70F, respectively. High temperatures during flowering 
can result in lower seed sets. The chickpea is relatively drought resistant because of its deep 
taproot. Therefore, chickpea cultivation is recommended on rainfed lands in Central Asia. 
Autumn-planted chickpeas mature before 15th June, while a spring-planted chickpea crop 
matures in July. In mountainous areas and colder regions where chickpeas are planted in April 
and May, the crop Matures in August and September.  
 
2.4.  Soil requirements 
Chickpeas can be grown on diverse soil types; however, well-drained loam and clay loam 
soils that are neutral to alkaline (pH 6.0 to 9.0) and have good water-holding capacity are best 
suited for chickpea. It is not suited to saline soils and it does not tolerate wet soils. It 
shouldn’t be planted in low-lying areas of fields where flooding conditions may occur.  
2.5.  Agronomic practices 
 
2.5.1.  Land preparation for planting 
Land preparation for sowing chickpeas depends on the soil type and cropping system. In the 
case of heavy soil, a rough seedbed is prepared to avoid packing  the cloddy surface due to 

5 
winter rains and to facilitate soil aeration and easy seedling emergence. It is necessary to 
deep-plow the field when autumn rains start. This opens the soil deep and ensures efficient 
moisture conservation from rain and snow. Deep plowing also reduces wilting of chickpeas 
that tend to develop due to the presence of hardpans in the root zone. Application of 
minimum tillage is possible, especially considering conservation agricultural practices and 
preserving soil moisture under rainfed conditions.  
 
 
2.5.2.  Improved varieties 
There are three new chickpea varieties that are suitable for planting both in autumn and 
spring. These are Abad, Malhotra, and Khalima. There are a few other varieties that are 
suitable for planting in spring only because they can’t survive cold winter temperatures in 
Uzbekistan.  
 
 
2.5.3.  Seeding time 
Winter chickpeas should be planted between October 15 and November 15 in the cold winter 
deserts. This allows the seedling establishment and root system development before the onset 
of winter. If a well-established winter chickpea seedling is killed during winter due to

Seeding of chickpeas should be done as early as possible in spring to utilize moist soil 
following winter rain and melting snow. However, soil temperature should be 10⁰C minimum 
average to ensure smooth germination. Planting the crop early in the spring is particularly 
advantageous under rainfed conditions, where limited late-season moisture has a strong 
negative impact on seed yield. As the crop requires a long growing season to mature and is 
frost tolerant, it can be planted in February, March, or early April depending on soil 
temperatures and moisture. It should be noted that in all areas, later plantings result in 
reduced yields and problems with crop drying before harvest. Also, grain quality may be 
reduced in some varieties by late planting, particularly for late-maturing varieties. 
2.5.4. Seed rate 
Seeing is done at the rate of 75-100 kg/ha depending upon seed size seeded at 8-10 cm depth. 
If the soil is well moist, a shallower seeding depth of 5 cm could be done. The decision on the 
seed rate should take into account the size of the selected seed lot. The recommended density 
is 40 to 45 plants per square meter. Seeds can be broadcasted or planted in rows of 45 to 60 
cm depending on the type of planting and harvesting method (manual or mechanical). Soil 
should be compacted after seeding to increase its contact with the seed for smooth 
germination. 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
An optimal plant stand is required for the desired yield level. A lower seed rate or poor-
quality seed can result in poor plant stand (Figure 5). Poor plant standing can also result in 
deeper seeding than normal depth, poor soil health or soil moisture deficit.

2.5.5. Seed treatment 
Seeds should be treated with a suitable fungicide mixture before planting, particularly when 
planting on cool soil. This protects the seed from seed and soil-borne diseases. If seed 
inoculant of Rhizobia species is available, seeds should be moistened with water, then mixed 
with the inoculant until all seeds are coated. Inoculated seeds should be kept under shade 
until they are planted. Seed treatment with bacterial inoculants allows more efficient 
atmospheric nitrogen through improved nodule formation. 
Inoculation is recommended under three different conditions, i) the legume to be planted was 
never grown or hasn’t been grown in the field for more than five years; ii) the legume was 
grown but the level of nodulation or efficiency of nitrogen fixation is not known; and iii) the 
land was never in cultivation such as desert or rangeland. If any of these conditions apply, the 
native rhizobia are probably not present in sufficient numbers to adequately modulate the 
crop, or they may not be efficient in fixing nitrogen.  
2.5.6. Nutrient Management 
Chickpea being a leguminous crop fulfills the major part of their nitrogen requirement (about 
75 percent) through the process of symbolic nitrogen fixation which works effectively from 
three to four weeks after sowing. However, soils with low organic matter and poor nitrogen 
supply may require 20-25 kg per hectare of nitrogen as the starter does, which can meet plant 
requirements because of the formation of nodules. Besides nitrogen, chickpeas respond very 
favorably to phosphorus application if the soils are deficient in phosphorous supply. If both 
nitrogen and phosphorus are required to be supplied, then diammonium phosphate (18-46-0) 
at the rate of 100 to 150 kg per hectare should be mixed in the soil uniformly before planting.  
2.5.7. Water Management 
Chickpea is mostly sown as a rainfed crop. However, where irrigation facilities are available, 
pre-sowing irrigation is needed to ensure proper germination and smooth crop growth. 
Depending on soil moisture, one irrigation at the pre-flowering stage and one at the pod 
development stage is recommended. Excess irrigation should be avoided, as it enhances 
vegetative growth and de-presses yield.  
2.5.8. Weed Management 
Chickpea suffers severely from the infestation of weeds (Figure 6). Weeds are a bigger 
problem for winter chickpeas compared to spring chickpeas. Weeds can be controlled either 
by using a hand hoe or wheel-hoe or by chemical herbicides. Weed control using hand-hoe 
should be preferred wherever possible as it improves aeration in the soil. 
One to two hand hoeing is needed depending upon weed infestation. Chickpea fields should 
be monitored for the appearance of weeds. Weeds should be controlled as early as possible to 
reduce their competition with crops for nutrients and moisture.

Chemical control of weed is possible either in pre-emergence or post-emergence. For 
chemical weed control, Fluchloralin (Basalin) should be sprayed at the rate of 1 kg per 
hectare in 800-1000 liters of water as a pre-planting spray herbicide. It should be well 
incorporated into the soil before sowing the crop. In case Basalin is not available, Metribuzin 
or Prometrynen can be sprayed at the rate of 1.0-1.5 kg active ingredient in 800-1000 liters of 
water per hectare as pre-emergence spray. For spraying herbicide on the standing crop, the 
type of weeds should be identified and a suitable herbicide available locally should be 
sprayed as recommended by the chemical manufacturer.

Chickpea crops can be attacked by a number of diseases and insect pests. The major diseases 
and their control are given below. 
Wilt ( Fusarium orthocerus L.) 
i) Treat the seed with Benlate T or a mixture of Benlate of Thiram (1:1) at the rate of 2.5 
g per kg of seed.

i) Grow resistant varieties such as Abad, Malhotra and Khalima.

2.7 Harvesting 
The crop becomes ready for harvest when leaves turn reddish-brown and start shedding 
(Figure 7). Plants are either plucked out by hand or cut with a sickle or machine harvested 
with a combine. If hand-harvested, the crop is allowed to dry in the sun on threshing floor for 
about five to six days. Thereafter, the threshing is done either by beating the plants with 
sticks or by using a stationary threshing machine or combine. 

10 
It is important to harvest chickpeas at the right stage of maturity and not delay harvesting to 
avoid shattering. Substantial grain losses can occur due to shattering at the time of harvest. If 
the harvested crop has to be taken out of the field and moved to a distant threshing floor, the 
harvested plants should be placed inside sacks to avoid grain loss during transportation 
(Figure 8). 
 
2.8. Storing and preserving 
i) Store in a cool, dry place for up to a year.  
ii) Management of bruchids (Callosobruchus chinensis)  
The adult and grub feed on the chickpea grain by making a small hole. Infested stored seeds 
can be recognized by the presence of white eggs on the seed surface and the round exit holes 
with the 'flap' of the seed coat. Kabuli types are particularly susceptible.

PREFACE
These guidelines are intended to provide countries with the appropriate statistical methodology 
and tools to properly estimate on- and off-farm losses for horticultural crops at the national and 
subnational levels. The proposed methodology follows a sample-based survey approach that can 
be either used as stand-alone surveys or articulated with the agricultural production surveys and 
other surveys along with the statistical data collection systems of each country.
The proposed approach should allow countries to improve their estimates of food losses and 
accordingly improve the estimation of food losses at the global level, which constitutes one indicator 
of Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) Subindicator 12.3.1.a – Global Food Loss Index, related to 
SDG 12 (“Ensure sustainable consumption and production patterns”) and Target 12.3 (“By 2030, to 
halve per capita global food waste at the retail and consumer levels and reduce food losses along 
production and supply chains, including post-harvest losses.”).
The core of the methodology proposed in these guidelines was field tested in Mexico under the 
supervision of INEGI with the technical support of ICAR–IASRI and FAO. These tests involved the 
combination of face-to-face interviews and direct field measurements to estimate food loss.

1.1 CONTEXT
Healthy, sustainable, and inclusive food systems are critical to achieving the world’s development 
goals. Agricultural development is one key area of intervention to end extreme poverty (Ivanic 
and Martin, 2018; Christiaensen and Martin, 2018), boost shared prosperity and feed a projected 
10 billion people by 2050. Agriculture accounts for 4 percent of global gross domestic product 
(GDP) and in some countries, it can account for more than 25 percent of GDP (FAO, 2025). Most 
of the world’s population depends on agriculture for their livelihood, especially people living in 
rural areas in developing countries. In addition to cereal and pulses, other crops such as fruits, 
vegetables, together with fish, meat and milk, are essential parts of a complete and balanced 
human diet.
Increasing global population and rising pressures on natural resources, such as arable land 
and water, as well as raising agricultural production to meet food demand, has become a major 
challenge for farmers and decision makers. Increasing productivity, especially in areas where it is 
largely below potential, is one option to fulfil this objective. Reducing harvest and post-harvest 
losses during different on-farm and off-farm operations can also improve the availability of food and 
the sustainability of food systems. To assess the potential to reduce losses at the different points of 
the value chain, relevant estimates and precise food loss data at the national level for major crops 
need to be established. Measuring post-harvest losses is crucial for understanding food supply, 
identifying loss mechanisms, and determining affected groups. This information helps shape policies 
to increase food availability, improve food security, enhance producer competitiveness, optimize 
supply chains and strengthen resilience against economic and climate shocks, especially in low- 
and middle-income countries where agriculture is a primary livelihood and losses are estimated 
to be significant (50x2030, 2021). In 2018, the Global Strategy to Improve Agricultural and Rural 
Statistics (GSARS) published the Guidelines on the measurement of harvest and post-harvest 
losses for grains (GSARS, 2018a). To date, no comprehensive methodology on the measurement of 
losses for horticultural products (fruits and vegetables) has been published.
Horticulture production is a rapidly growing subsector of agriculture in many countries, including 
in the household sector, where it provides an additional and often sizeable source of income and 
livelihoods. Horticultural crops, a source of vitamins, minerals and antioxidants, are essential for 
a nutritionally balanced diet. Losses of perishable crops tend to be relatively high as fruits and 
vegetables are more susceptible to deterioration due to biological and environmental factors. Such 
biological factors include respiration rate, ethylene production, compositional changes, growth and 
development, transpiration, physiological breakdown, physical damage, pathological breakdown, 
surface area to volume and membrane permeability. Environmental factors include pests, 
microbial infection, natural ripening processes, heat, drought and improper post-harvest handling.

1.2 OBJECTIVES, GOALS AND GENERAL APPROACH OF THE GUIDELINES
The objective of this document is to address this lack of a comprehensive methodology on 
the measurement of horticultural losses through the proposal of an operationally feasible and 
statistically sound approach to measure harvest and post-harvest losses for fruits and vegetables 
at the country level.
These guidelines are mostly intended for offices in charge of producing and compiling agricultural 
statistics, but their recommendations are valid in other settings as well, including for research 
and academic institutions. They present conceptual and operational recommendations on the  
implementation of sample-based approaches to measure food losses on the farm and along 
the supply chain (off the farm) for horticultural crops at the country level. The methodology will 
be useful to national statistics offices, ministries of agriculture and other line ministries responsible 
for measuring and monitoring food losses, planning bodies, administrative bodies, associations/
boards, research institutes and other national institutions seeking sound data on food losses 
along supply chains and across the fruits and vegetables food group. At the regional or national 
level, the guidelines will prove valuable for identifying and prioritizing national and subnational 
policies and interventions and monitoring progress towards the reduction of losses. Eventually, it 
can also inform larger agricultural development and investment projects.
The focus of the guidelines is on sample surveys. These surveys are proposed to attain reliable 
and representative national estimates of food losses at a relatively low cost especially if countries 
leverage their existing agricultural surveys and other available national surveys and information 
systems. Adjoining a loss module to existing surveys also makes it possible to improve the 
relevance of the data and its usability by expanding the analytical possibilities. The assessment 
methods proposed in the guidelines combine physical (or objective) measurements with 
declarations. Indeed, as each of these two approaches are affected by specific (and mostly 
uncorrelated) biases, the combination of these estimates can reduce the overall estimation bias.
While sample surveys are the statistical approach recommended in the guidelines, complementary 
methods are also mentioned. These methods may be used in combination with survey data to  
improve the quality of the final estimates or as preliminary studies prior to full-scale surveys to  
identify the critical loss points and to better target and calibrate the survey instrument. In order to 
design relevant and applicable interventions and facilitate the selection of effective and efficient 
solutions, in-depth assessments and impact analysis might be necessary, which identify feasible 
business cases of loss reduction and their trade-offs along the supply chain. These are further 
described in the State of Food and Agriculture report of 2019 (FAO, 2019b).
The methodology is designed in such a way that it can be used to measure losses in different 
stages, covering a wide range of perishable products. Starting at the harvest, it can cover the full 
post-harvest chain, including the farm, transportation, storage, processing and distribution stages. 
The questions have been tailored to fruits and vegetables. They are organized by operations or 
activities so that the final questionnaire design for each stage is flexible and can measure losses for 
the relevant activities in that stage, for that specific product and country.

1.3 CHARACTERISTICS OF THE FIELD TESTS
A series of field tests were conducted to assess the feasibility of the approach presented in 
these guidelines to measure losses on and off the farm for fruits and vegetables using a sample 
survey strategy. The tests involved the entire data production cycle, from selecting the sample 
to conducting interviews, taking physical measurements and processing the data, and compiling 
the final extrapolations.
The tests were conducted in Mexico by the National Statistics Office (INEGI), with technical support 
from the Indian Council for Agricultural Research–Indian Agricultural Statistics Research Institute 
(ICAR–IASRI), the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) Partnership and 
Liaison Office in Mexico and the Statistics Division of FAO. Two states were selected for the pilot 
exercise: Jalisco, in which the municipality of Cihuatlán was selected as the site for testing the 
data collection instruments of fruit losses on banana, and Guanajuato, in which Dolores Hidalgo 
and Valle de Santiago were the two municipalities selected for the assessment of vegetable losses, 
focusing on broccoli.
After IASRI conducted a comprehensive training of the enumerators, a sample of 70 fruit-growing 
households (20 selected for physical measurements, in addition to the interview) and 140 
vegetable-growing households (40 for physical measurements) was drawn from the existing listings 
in the three municipalities. The data collection and physical measurements were carried out in 
September and October 2018 with tablets and computer-assisted questionnaires using Survey 
Solutions, a software application adapted to conduct electronic surveys.
Some of the lessons learned and the main results of these field tests are presented throughout 
this document to illustrate the methodology proposed, to point out certain challenges faced during 
the data collection or to present final estimates. The field test report was published on the FAO 
Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) Subindicator 12.3.1.a website.1
In general, the field test showed that declarations seem to underestimate losses compared with 
physical measurement, as already highlighted in cereals and pulses (GSARS, 2018a). A clear 
assessment of the handling system along the different stages of the supply chain and detailed 
training of enumerators was seen to be key. The volatility of losses from one week to the other, 
which is often observed in rural aggregation and wholesaler stages, was a major challenge to 
measuring losses, whether through interviews and respondents reporting on their losses, or 
through physical measurements.

2.1 SCOPE IN TERMS OF FOOD LOSS, CROPS AND THE VALUE CHAIN
This document is focused on food losses along the supply chain, on and off the farm, for horticultural 
crops. In this section, the scope of these guidelines is described by setting the reference definition 
and representation of the value chain.
Food loss definition:
It is acknowledged in the guidelines that many definitions of food losses exist. The general 
conceptual definition established by FAO in 2014 states that “food losses are the reduction in 
quantity and quality of food”. For measurement purposes at the national level consistent with 
SDG Subindicator 12.3.1.a, an operational definition for measurement purposes was approved, 
focusing on quantitative losses only and which applies to horticultural crops:
“Food losses are all the crop and livestock human-edible commodity quantities that, directly 
or indirectly, completely exit the post-harvest/slaughter production/supply chain by being 
discarded, incinerated or otherwise, and do not re-enter in any other utilization (such as 
animal feed, industrial use, etc.), up to, and excluding, the retail level. Losses that occur during 
storage, transportation, and processing also of imported quantities, are therefore all included. 
Losses include the commodity as a whole with its non-edible parts.” (FAO, 2018)2
Qualitative losses are not in the scope of SDG Subindicator 12.3.1.a – Food Loss Index, as these 
would burden countries with the responsibility of reporting towards the indicator. Also, they are 
complex to measure and to standardize at the national and international level. Nevertheless, 
qualitative deterioration can be critical in fruits and vegetables supply chains; therefore, countries 
can decide to add modules for measuring it to the approaches presented here. No specific guidance 
will be provided on qualitative losses in these guidelines but approaches from the literature can be 
consulted (Mohammed et al., 2018) to complement these if the country wishes to do so.
In practice, the boundaries along the supply chain are not so clear-cut, and there can be grey 
areas that affect the measurements. Additional considerations can be found in Chapter 5 about 
the downstream stages of the value chain (off the farm).
Crop coverage:
The guidelines cover all horticultural crops, i.e. fruits and vegetables. These crops may have 
an annual or infra-annual cycle, such as tomatoes and lettuce or constitute permanent or 
semi-permanent production, such as tree crops (oranges, bananas, etc.).

Within each of the stages, on and off the farm, various activities and operations are conducted. 
These activities depend on the type of produce and the level of sophistication of the value chain. 
For fruits and vegetables, food supply chains typically include the following: harvesting; on-farm 
handling, which includes collection, transfer to harvesting containers, cleaning, pre-cooling, 
sorting, grading, post-harvest treatments, packaging, storage and transportation on the farm to the 
packinghouse. Off-the-farm actors in the different stages, namely aggregators/collection centres/
packinghouses, processing industry, wholesalers conduct the same handling operations as above 
(i.e. sorting, grading, packaging, processing, storage, and transportation, among others), although 
with various degrees of specialization. Further details are given throughout the guidelines.
Scope in terms of edible and non-edible parts:
Any methodology that measures food must be clear about the treatment of non-edible parts. In the 
SDG methodology and in these guidelines, food loss measurements are made on the commodities 
in their primary state, also known as primary products. In other words, food losses are made of 
the edible and non-edible parts of the produce, for example a fruit that is discarded as a whole. 
When the non-edible parts are removed along the supply chain, the guidelines provide some 
rules to be consistent with the basic principles that measure primary products. When non-edible 
parts are removed, for example during trimming and processing, the loss of input products is 
considered as a whole, while only the loss of edible parts removed during processing are measured 
as such. These must be converted into their primary product equivalent to account for weight 
loss (e.g. the removed peel of oranges). As the distinction between edible and non-edible parts 
can be difficult and might depend on the perception of an individual, all removed quantities can 
be captured in the measurement as removed parts, and coefficients of non-edible parts applied 
afterwards. Given that most fruits and vegetables arrive at the retail and consumer stages as a 
whole and considering that almost all parts of fruits and vegetables can be considered as edible, 
there might be very few cases where these considerations are crucial.

Food: Any substance, whether processed, semi-processed or raw, which is intended for human

Delays (waiting): This occurs when conditions do not permit or do not require the immediate

can also include different options related to the management of food loss and waste as landfill,

waste management are considered food losses.
Economic losses: Income that was not generated due to quantitative losses and qualitative 
losses. In qualitative losses, the crop is often diverted to secondary markets or other economic 
uses, where lower prices are paid.6, 7

Economic sectors: Consist of a coherent and consistent classification structure of economic activities

Edible and non-edible parts: Edible refers to the parts of food that a population of a specific

Fit for human consumption: Edible produce that meets the general standards designed to assure

Food waste: In the SDG framework, food waste is the food quantities removed at the retail level,

Food supply chain: A specific type of agricultural supply chain comprised of commodities intended 
to be produced and sold for human consumption. Non-food agricultural products (e.g. fibres) are 
not included. The main actors in the stages of a food supply chain are farmers, intermediaries 
(including agents, assemblers or collectors and transporters), processors, wholesalers (including 
importers and exporters), retailers and final consumers.
Grading: Classification of the commodity based on quality standards accepted by the industry and 
markets, for example, graded produce can be classified as Extra Class, Class I or Class II.
Harvest loss: Quantity of produce lost during harvesting operations, such as mature or quasi-mature 
damaged (cut, broken, ruptured) and undamaged fruits and vegetables left in the field during harvest. 
Harvest losses include only the commodities that are mature or quasi-mature but not losses that 
have occurred because of environmental factors before the produce was ready for harvest.
Off-farm: All stages, operations and activities involving the produce after it leaves the farm, 
i.e. when the farmer no longer owns or handles the product.
On-farm: All operations and activities carried out by the farmer on the farm, until the product is 
consumed directly on the farm or exits the farm gate. This may include activities the farmer does 
directly on the farm, i.e. at home or at the packinghouse, but the famer still owns or handles 
the product.
Operations/activities: Those activities performed by an actor on the product. For example, the act 
of harvesting a product, reception, trimming, pre-cooling, washing, sorting and grading, waxing, 
packaging, storing and transporting. Some activities apply to more than one stage.
Packinghouse: A packinghouse is a physical structure where harvested produce is consolidated 
and prepared for transport and distribution to markets. Activities before and after packing are called 
packinghouse operations, and they include receiving and recording, cleaning, sorting/grading, 
pre-treatments, packing, cooling, storage and dispatch to markets. A packinghouse enables the 
quality assurance activities that ensure the product quality and quantity meet market requirements 
and that losses are minimized during transport and distribution to markets. Furthermore, a 
packinghouse can serve as a hub for coordination and governance of a farm-packinghouse-market 
organization in which market demand dictates production and activities. The activities depend

on the type of produce and market. Therefore, the packinghouse is the site where post-harvest 
treatments are applied, and quality standards are monitored. Packing can be an activity that takes 
place in different stages of the value chain, or it can also be done by a separate actor that needs 
to be surveyed.
Post-harvest loss: This is the reduction in the quantity of produce that is observed after harvesting 
up to, but excluding, the retail level. These losses are the result of a variety of causes during 
post-harvest operations, such as mishandling and improper storage.
Pre-cooling: The main purpose of pre-cooling is to remove the field heat from the crops after they 
are harvested. Pre-cooling reduces the fruit respiration rate, extends crop storage life and inhibits 
the growth of various microorganisms, thus reducing the possibility of post-harvest diseases.
Pre-harvest loss: The pre-harvest loss of horticultural crops refers to the reduction in quantity of 
crops that occurs before harvest, when the produce is not suitable anymore for harvesting. This 
can be caused by various factors such as environmental conditions (temperature, relative humidity, 
water potential, light), cultural practices and pest management techniques.
Processing loss: The quantity of whole produce, parts of the produce and final products 
removed and discarded while food is being processed. This includes produce that is graded and 
discarded before processing, final products that are discarded, as well as edible parts that are 
removed and discarded during processing. All loss quantities must be converted into the primary 
product equivalent.
Qualitative losses: The decrease of quality attributes of food that results in the reduction of 
nutritional value, economic value, food safety or consumer appreciation (FAO, 2014).
Quantitative losses: The decrease in food mass along of the supply chain.8
Retail markets: These are markets directly serving consumers in the main urban areas, such as 
provincial, town and city centres. Although primarily retail, they may have some semi-wholesale 
functions, particularly if they allow farmers to trade. In that case, they are often called farmers 
markets. This operation is very typical in developing countries, but there has also been a strong 
global trend to create farmers markets to sell specialized produce, such as organically grown fruits 
and vegetables (FAO, 2014).
Sorting and sizing: Sorting is the process of separating different types of fruits or vegetables 
based on size, shape, colour and quality (freedom from defects and damage). In particular, sizing 
is the classification of produce into different categories based on dimensions. Sizing can be based 
on weight, diameter or length.
Storage loss: This is the reduction in the quantity of produce that occurs during storage and 
is caused by pest infestation and rodents, for example.
Storage: This takes place when the product is intentionally placed in a specific location to protect 
it from adverse conditions or to hold it until it can be marketed.

Boundaries between pre-harvest, harvest and post-harvest losses for fruits and vegetables are not 
straightforward, as they may vary considerably depending on the production system and harvest 
practice (open field vs greenhouse; manual vs mechanical, or number of pickings). Accordingly, 
additional analysis may be required for the survey design and data collection to understand 
these boundaries.

or are lost during the harvest process itself. Post-harvest losses are those loss quantities that are

were caused. While Table 1 provides a general orientation, it might still be insufficient to clearly 
distinguish between harvest and pre-harvest losses in some specific circumstances. Box 1 provides

Produce that is not harvested due to weather, pest and disease-related damage
One crucial challenge is how to treat produce that has been damaged by weather, pests and diseases. In some instances, 
the produce is a total loss with no economic value, while in others the produce can still be used for secondary markets, such as

limiting when the larger parts of the produce are left on the field at harvest because of severe weather or pest damage (partial

Produce that is not harvested due to market conditions and opportunity costs
For fruits and vegetables, it is more commonly observed that plots or orchards are not harvested for market or economic 
reasons. As highlighted in this section, such causes of food loss as overproduction, market dynamics, low prices and harvest 
costs are some of the most common factors prompting producers to not harvest their produce. In food loss assessments, these 
are considered either harvest or pre-harvest losses depending on the studies. However, the cause of loss occurs downstream 
in the supply chain (markets) and the decision to turn mature produce into a loss is taken at harvest, so conceptually these 
are harvest losses. The related measurement challenge is that the question on harvest losses in the questionnaire does not 
capture these losses because of their pre-harvest characteristic (the plot was not harvested at all). As these losses can be 
policy-relevant, a specific question on unharvested areas/plots and their causes, one being markets and price conditions, should 
be included in surveys. Most agricultural surveys already include a question on unharvested areas, but do not ask for the reason.
All mature produce is harvested
When all mature produce is harvested independently of the quality standards, and if it is damaged or infested, food losses occur 
during sorting and grading after harvesting and are generally assigned to post-harvest losses. There are different  reasons for 
harvesting all mature produce, such as having access to markets for second and third quality produce, where lower quality 
produce still has an economic value, or the use of harvesting machines that do not allow for sorting on the field. The possibility 
of selling the produce to the processing industry is often a reason to harvest all mature produce. In this scenario, harvest 
losses can be relatively low compared with post-harvest losses.
Only mature produce that fits quality standards is harvested
In other cases, the produce is sorted at harvest and damaged/infested produce or produce that does not meet quality standards 
(size, colour or shape) is left on the field, even if it is still fit for human consumption. The produce left in the field is normally 
reintegrated into the soil. These food losses are assigned to harvest losses and – compared with the scenario in which all mature 
produce is harvested – post-harvest losses in grading are expected to be lower. There are several reasons for grading during 
harvesting; one of them is when the destination market (e.g. fresh and export markets) sets high-quality standards with premium 
prices and no secondary uses are given. In this case, cost-efficiency is the main reason for leaving the produce on the field.
All produce is harvested and used
The most common scenario is to observe losses at the farm level, either during harvesting or during post-harvest operations. 
For example, if business operations are highly diversified, farmers will harvest all fruits and vegetables at varying stages of 
maturity. The immature produce is used for pickles and other secondary processed products, the fully mature go to wholesale 
and the ripe mature go to local markets. Some products are harvested green or semi-mature, such as bananas, avocados and 
mangos, for the export markets. Overripe fruits are used for tertiary processing options like juice, jams, nectars or dried fruits. 
In such cases, harvest and post-harvest losses are negligible.
Prolonged harvesting periods with multiple pickings
For crops with prolonged harvest periods, harvesting encompasses multiple pickings. In general, only mature crops that 
fit the desired quality standards are picked. Losses then consist of the mature fruits not harvested in the corresponding 
period when it would be picked or produce that falls when it is being picked and is left on the ground. In some cases, (more 
common in greenhouses), the plots are cleaned between picking periods. The cleaning operation removes the produce that 
does not meet quality standards or that is damaged. These are considered as harvest losses. After the last picking period, 
the remaining produce is either withdrawn and disposed of (greenhouses) or reincorporated into the soil (open field).
In this kind of scenario, the producer may not wish to pick the produce at the very end because the quality is poor and is not 
profitable, and the field is thereby abandoned until the next crop is planted. In such cases, the produce that is left in the field 
has to be considered as a harvest loss.
Theft before harvest
There are realistic situations where the crop on the plot achieved maturity and attained all market quality requirements but 
is subjected to praedial larceny or theft. Since the crop was not harvested by the legitimate producer, it is not considered 
a quantitative loss as the crop will be sold and consumed during the post-production period. It is, though, an economic loss 
for the farmers that will be captured by lower yields.

2.4 CAUSES OF FOOD LOSSES IN FRUITS AND VEGETABLES
Fruits and vegetables are lost for various reasons. The quality of fresh produce as it initially enters 
the food supply chain has a significant impact on the quality of the final product when marketed 
to consumers. Quality cannot be improved but only maintained as the produce moves through the 
food supply chain. Thus, a major objective of the food supply chain must be to minimize quantity 
and quality losses in balance with the economic realities of the marketplace.
Pre-harvest factors, particularly cultural practices, are considered the gateway for post-harvest 
quality and storage disease management. The quality of horticultural products at harvest has a 
major effect on its post-harvest life. Once the quality is compromised, due to inefficient pre-harvest 
practices and the presence of disease inoculum during growth and development, it is difficult 
to maintain the desired level of high quality after harvest. Irrigation, rootstock, training, pruning, 
fertilizer application and other cultural practices such as plant protection affect the nutrient and 
water supply to the plant, which can affect the hidden quality attributes (acidity, total soluble solids, 
vitamins, minerals, carbohydrates) and the visual quality characteristics, such as the shape, size, 
colour, flavour and glossiness of the produce. The exposure of fruits and vegetables to fungi, bacteria 
and viruses during growth determines the extent of incidence and severity of post-harvest diseases 
at handling and storage. Among the three basic strategies of disease management (latent infection 
elimination, prevention of infection on surface and within-host tissues), pre-harvest practices are 
the most important for the effective control of post-harvest diseases (Asrey and Das, 2021).
In addition, farmers may harvest their crops prematurely, especially fruits and vegetables, due to 
poverty and the urgent need for food and cash because of market demand, a deliberate fire or out 
of insecurity and fear of theft. Immature fruits are more prone to mechanical damage, shrivelling, 
loss in appearance, chilling injury and loss in fresh weight and, ultimately, are less profitable. 
Conversely, while waiting for market opportunities, farmers may decide to harvest over mature 
fruits and vegetables, with limited shelf life. Additionally, physical damage due to bruises, abrasions, 
punctures, compression during harvesting would cause subsequent losses at later stages in the 
handling system arising from secondary infections and water stress, which would eventually result 
in increased respiration and ethylene production rates enhancing senescence.
In the opposite scenario, farmers might harvest green or semi-mature fruits as they are less prone 
to losses during transportation, handling, etc. This is common for fruits like bananas, avocados, 
mangos, papayas, pineapples, etc., especially when they are aimed at the export market.
Failure to properly cool crops immediately after harvest from a warm field could reduce their shelf 
life and increase post-harvest losses of perishable crops. Pre-cooling is an essential operation 
aimed at removing heat from fresh produce immediately after harvest and before they are sent 
to storage. This practice is encouraged to reduce fruit respiration and ethylene production rates 
and extend the shelf life of the fresh produce. It is also effective in reducing post-harvest diseases 
by inhibiting the growth of various micro-organisms. In many developing countries, perishable 
produce is packed in deep, poorly ventilated, dark-coloured containers after harvest, uncovered at 
field collection points and exposed to high ambient temperatures, which contributes to heat injuries 
and food losses particularly when unexpected delays are encountered, and this is compounded by 
poor logistical arrangements.

2. Conceptual framework
19
Multiple handling of fresh produce can lead to quality deterioration and post-harvest losses. 
The extent of the damage depends on the type of produce, the handling method and the duration of 
storage. Some common causes of quality deterioration include mechanical damage, temperature 
abuse and exposure to ethylene gas. In some cases, high appearance standards may divert food 
that is perfect for human consumption into other uses that are less profitable. Also, it should be 
noted that if infrastructure for initial storage is lacking, perishable produce can spoil within hours.
Along with the supply chain, fruits and vegetables that are poorly packed or packaged for storage, 
transportation and market display incur physical damage and losses in quality. Crushing lower-level 
containers in stacks of produce is a major packaging-related problem. This could be due to 
container side walls being incapable of supporting the stacking heights, containers becoming 
weakened by excessive moisture, excess weight and stress being placed upon containers due to 
improper stacking or loading methods. Underfilling and overfilling containers can ultimately result in 
compression damage on lower layers of stacked cartons. Additionally, containers with inadequate 
ventilation can obstruct the cold air flow around produce, which could slow the cooling rate.
In rural areas (where most production takes place), inadequate road networks often lead to 
transportation damage that lowers both the quality and value of fresh produce, while also driving 
up logistics costs. These challenges become even more severe during the rainy season, as 
water further deteriorates the roads and increased vibrations may damage both the produce and 
vehicles. This results in higher transportation expenses, damaged goods, and ultimately reduced 
income for farmers. The losses can be both qualitative, such as visual blemishes on the produce, 
and quantitative, with some crops made unmarketable due to the damage. Both producers 
and wholesalers may share these financial burdens, depending on who owns the goods during 
transport. Additionally, mechanical injuries from poor roads can trigger negative physiological and 
morphological changes in horticultural products, including colour changes in the peel, softening 
or cracking. In most developing countries, the processing facilities are inadequate. Processing 
industries often lack the capacity to process the volumes delivered. Seasonality in the production 
of produce can have a significant impact on processed products. Seasonal fluctuations in 
consumer demand and inconsistent ingredient supply represent significant challenges for beverage 
manufacturers. Accurate forecasting becomes difficult when sales and supply chain performance 
are influenced by seasonality. Although most businesses recognize the adverse impact of these 
fluctuations, many lack adequate strategies to address them. Excess supply of fresh produce 
during peak periods, especially when paired with lower demand for processed products, can lead 
to substantial losses for processors.
Continuous cropping (the repeated cultivation of the same crop or crops from the same botanical 
family on a given plot across successive seasons without rotation) should be documented as part 
of the contextual data collected during harvest and post-harvest loss assessments. Continuous 
cropping is associated with higher soil-borne pest and disease pressure, nutrient depletion, and 
declining yield potential, all of which can increase both visible field losses (e.g. rejected or damaged 
produce at harvest) and latent losses that only manifest during storage or transport (e.g. rot, internal 
breakdown, or reduced shelf life). Where possible, data collectors should record the cropping history 
of the sampled plot (including whether rotation, fallow periods, or cover cropping were practiced 
in preceding seasons) alongside standard loss measurements. This information allows analysts 
to distinguish losses attributable to handling, storage, or transport practices from those due to 
pre-harvest agronomic conditions, and supports a more accurate interpretation of loss percentages 
relative to a plot’s yield potential rather than its actual, possibly already reduced, harvest.

3.1 GENERAL ASPECTS
There are several possible approaches for the measurement and estimation of food losses at the 
national level, with each one serving specific purposes. To estimate food loss at the country level, 
as required for compiling SDG Subindicator 12.3.1.a, these guidelines recommend using probability 
sample surveys as the backbone of any loss measurement at the national level. These surveys, 
however, can be complemented by other estimation methods for cost-efficiency. The different 
approaches are usually separated into indirect and direct methods, with their respective advantages 
and limitations regarding the monitoring of food losses at the country level. Box 2 provides a 
general description of the various approaches.
Box 2. General approaches for food loss measurement at the country level
Indirect approaches for food loss measurement
Indirect methods have lower resource requirements than direct approaches, as food losses are estimated based on models or 
balance-mass methods using available data. Thus, indirect loss estimation does not require or reduce data collection needs. 
They are estimation methods or analytical techniques that produce loss estimates based on different data sources, parameters 
and assumptions. Model assumptions cover the relationship between the dependent variable (losses) and independent 
variables, such as weather conditions, crop variety, prices and farming practices; in a mass-balance approach, the balances 
between macroeconomic variables such as the supply and utilization accounts determine the relationship and the loss levels.
A mass-balance approach was used in Global food loss and waste – Extent, causes and prevention, 1 based on the supply and 
utilization accounts used to estimate food supply, where food losses were estimated indirectly as a residual.
A model-based approach is currently used to estimate the global SDG Subindicator 12.3.1.a – Food Loss Index, with an 
econometric model developed by FAO that estimates food losses at the global and regional level from available food balance 
sheet data, country reports, food loss estimates screened from peer-reviewed and grey literature compiled in the FAO Food Loss 
and Waste database, and a set of explanatory variables.2 Models, however, require sufficient and reliable input data to estimate 
the model's parameters. Data is still too scarce in most cases to allow for reliable country-level estimates, and SDG estimates 
can only be published at the regional and global level. These modelled estimates are used as placeholders in the short term, 
while data collection efforts are being put in place to produce more survey-based estimates in the medium term.
Model-based approaches can be useful also at the micro level or on a smaller scale, where data availability is higher or 
data collection is feasible in the short term (for instance, to estimate the relation between type of storage facility, weather 
conditions and grain losses). These can then be used in combination with survey data to improve results.
Indirect approaches, therefore, are useful to complement direct approaches when survey-based data is not sufficient.

Aggregation frameworks to compile food loss from multiple data sources and collection points

These guidelines recommend using direct estimation methods requiring data collection (mainly 
sample-based surveys) where data accuracy is most important and relevant, i.e. in the critical

complementing these surveys with other cost-efficient methods (field trials, estimation models and

other aspects of the value chain, such as details on the causes of losses. 12 As data collection is 
resource-intensive, designing a new data collection system requires a strategy to optimize efforts 
and costs.
As losses occur along the whole supply chain, preliminary field visits to understand the supply 
chain of the selected crops help with strategizing data collection. The preliminary field visits will 
help to provide an overview of pre-harvest factors, a mapping and sequencing of all steps in the 
post-harvest handling system, the climatic conditions, the tools and practices, and the general 
characteristics of each stage of procedures, operations and treatments. To this end, FAO designed

losses and the critical loss points.

products and on the critical food loss points, and in these, identify the most cost-efficient estimation 
method that can produce sufficient sound data. The remaining data gaps, where no data collection 
will take place, can be filled with complementary and lighter approaches (rapid appraisal, expert 
opinion, focus groups, etc.).
Food losses are multidisciplinary by nature and span across many economic sectors. A coordination 
mechanism and technical working group will be needed for the success of the endeavour.

3.3 SAMPLE-BASED SURVEYS AND COMPLEMENTARY APPROACHES 
TO COLLECT FOOD LOSS DATA
3.3.1 Sample surveys as the backbone of food loss measurement at the national level
These guidelines recommend adopting a sample-survey approach for estimating food losses. 
Sample surveys are a major instrument of official statistics. Sample surveys in agriculture statistics 
are used to regularly estimate crop yields, area and production. Statistical surveys based on 
random samples produce statistically representative national or subnational estimates. In contrast 
to deterministic samples, random samples make it possible to assess the degree of precision of 
the estimates, such as standard errors and confidence intervals. This is an important feature given 
the high degree of volatility of agricultural variables and the fragmentation of the agricultural sector 
in most developing countries.
Agricultural sample-based surveys can be used as possible anchors for loss measurement. 
Subsamples of holdings and plots of the main agricultural survey can be drawn for the implementation 
of an additional loss module or questionnaire. This greatly improves the cost-effectiveness of the 
loss survey, the reliability of the estimates (which benefit from the large sample sizes generally 
used for agricultural surveys) and increases the usability of the information (losses can be analysed 
and explained using the variables collected by the main survey because they are statistically 
consistent). Mali and Ethiopia included loss modules in their national farm surveys; Mali starting 
with the 2021/2022 Enquête Agricole de Conjoncture (EAC) survey, 14 and Ethiopia in 2022 (FAO 
and ESS, 2023), while Malawi applied a special loss questionnaire on a subsample of their national 
agricultural survey in 2021 (Ministry of Agriculture, 2021).15 Loss assessments may also piggyback 
on national surveys covering stages off the farm. For example, DANE, the National Statistics Office 
in Colombia designed a loss module to be included in its national manufacturing survey and its 
commerce survey (DANE, 2021). INEGI, the National Statistics Office in Mexico, designed and 
conducted a pilot on a special supply chain survey to track prices and value addition along the 
supply chain of key agricultural products, including questions on food losses (INEGI, 2021). Other 
countries opt to establish a special ad hoc survey to measure food losses along the key supply 
chains. The most comprehensive example is the case of India, where IASRI conducted three rounds 
of the national food loss survey on 47 products (Jha et al., 2015).
The Global Strategy to Improve Agriculture and Rural Statistics developed a Handbook on crop 
statistics: improving methods for measuring crop area, production and yield (GSARS, 2018b) that 
supports the design and implementation of agricultural surveys and production of crop statistics. 
It examines various approaches for designing a probability sample for a crop production survey 
to identify a viable sampling strategy at the farm level, including the choice of a suitable sampling 
frame, sample size determination and allocation, selection methods, and estimation. Some sampling 
strategies and the estimation of probability surveys using three types of sampling frames – list, 
area, and multiple frames – are also presented. The handbook offers a solid reference to establish 
farm sampling from which to draw a subsample for post-harvest loss measurement.

3.3.3 Assessment methods to measure food loss in sample surveys
The assessment method 17 is the method used to quantify losses in a sample survey or other 
data collection instrument. The main methods used in surveys are declarations and physical 
measurements (see definitions in Section 2.2). Declarations by respondents are the standard and 
most frequent method used. Declarations are less resource intensive than physical measurements 
and other assessment methods but have limitations when the respondent has difficulty recalling 
the lost amounts or observing them directly. Physical measurements can be more accurate but 
are more resource intensive because they require an actual measurement, such as sorting and 
weighing, by the enumerator. These guidelines, therefore, recommend combining declarations with 
physical measurements, which will be further detailed in the rest of the document.

Visual scales are rating scales that use photographs of the fruits and vegetables with different 
qualitative grades. They are often used to evaluate the qualitative grade of the produce in surveys. 
The qualitative state of the produce before and after the handling process can be identified by 
the enumerator and respondent visually, therefore the visual scales help them to make qualitative 
evaluations in a quick and homogenous manner. In case studies or experimental designs on food 
losses of fruits and vegetables, these scales are used for qualitative loss assessments. It is likely 
that the lowest quality scale refers to produce that can no longer be sold or consumed, which 
can indicate that they will be discarded. Nevertheless, to be sure these are quantitative losses, 
a question should be added that confirms the quantity of produce that has been discarded.

conducted at the business level, by food processors themselves to be able to single out how much

Similarly, the use of diaries or records is less common in large sample-based surveys. Record 
sheets have to be handed over to respondents, for instance, in rural collection centres of food 
processors or wholesalers. Given the complex field operation of a national survey, it can be 
challenging for enumerators to follow up on the completion of diaries over a prolonged period, 
especially when respondents are scattered over large/remote areas. Additionally, response rates 
might be negatively affected when the reporting period is too long, thus affecting the sample size 
and requiring replacements, which can induce biases in the sample. Nevertheless, if respondents 
keep records to manage their losses, they will be able to provide more accurate declarations in 
the survey.
Mass balance is an assessment method whereby the difference between the input and the output 
quantity is the quantity lost (i.e. the quantity bought and sold by a wholesaler). In a sample survey, 
this assessment method would require including questions on the input and output quantity handled 
by the respondent in a certain period. However, given the wide recourse to reutilizations of the 
removed and unsold produce (sent to other secondary uses, reused internally for other by-products, 
sent to animal feed), a simple balance between input and output is often insufficient to infer losses. 
Rather, quantities sent to all potential destinations and re-utilizations will have to be considered 
and declared to avoid overestimating losses in a mass balance exercise. Moreover, to obtain the 
correct quantity lost, the secondary quantities must be accurate, which can be challenging for the 
respondent. A direct question on losses can be preferable. Nevertheless, questions on quantities 
sent to the different destinations and reutilizations can be asked in the survey in order to check if 
the declared loss is within an expected range. This can be relevant when the respondent might not 
know which destinations are part of loss or not (e.g. animal feeding versus composting).
3.3.4 Combining declarations and physical measurement in sample surveys 
for better quality data
These guidelines propose that declarations and physical measurement be combined to improve 
the accuracy of loss estimates in sample-based surveys. The literature review shows that loss 
estimates are highly variable for fruits and vegetables,19 even for a single crop in a given area. This is 
because losses of fruits and vegetables can be driven by a series of factors such as the cultivation 
system, the seasonality of production, supply fluctuations, the type of market and destination, and 
climatic conditions.
Declarations, by asking farmers, traders or other respondents to provide their own estimate of 
losses, may lead to distortions and biases, as losses might be difficult to observe, such as losses at 
harvest (farmers tend to concentrate on the harvested production and losses occurring at this stage 
are ignored or not apparent). Losses might also occur during operations where the respondent is 
not necessarily present (such as off-farm transport), or operations might span across a long period, 
such as apple storage, and is difficult to recall. It is also challenging to declare losses when the 
actors conduct repetitive operations during the reference period of the survey, with losses varying 
from one to the other, such as in wholesale markets, where market conditions and corresponding 
loss levels vary from week to week. Distortions are expected to be higher among farmers, especially 
the smaller ones, than among value chain actors, such as processors or wholesalers who tend 
to keep detailed records. However, stronger enumerator capacity via training and well-designed

3.3.5 Using qualitative assessment to design the sample surveys or fill data gaps
Given that food losses are a relatively new concept in national agricultural and economic surveys, 
it is highly recommended that a supply chain analysis be conducted prior to designing food loss 
surveys and modules. This helps to understand the supply chain, the different stages and actors, 
the set of operations they conduct, their characteristics and main loss points.
FAO’s food loss assessment method (FAO, 2019a) is a system for conducting a rapid appraisal of 
losses along the supply chain. It can serve as guidance on how to carry out a supply chain analysis 
to obtain first approximations of losses, identify critical loss points, the main causes of losses and 
possible solutions. Conducting an assessment this way can be done as a combination of desk 
study, expert consultations and field visits. This can be done effectively by post-harvest experts 
together with the technical teams in the line government institutions (ministry of agriculture, ministry 
of commerce, institutions in charge of small and medium enterprises or other institutions) and 
key stakeholders of the selected supply chains. Depending on the number of supply chains, the 
assessment can be done by two people in collaboration with the technical team in the government 
institution over a period of approximately two months.
As highlighted in Section 2, some specifications prior to the survey design might be needed to set 
the boundaries on the farm between pre-harvest, harvest and post-harvest losses. Similarly, the 
boundaries between different stages of the supply chain and the actors involved in each stage, the 
number of businesses per stage and their geographical dispersion might also require some expert 
orientation to set the sample design. To better tailor the questions on losses and the physical 
measurement, one must understand the different operations conducted by the actors and how 
losses occur in them. Additionally, some actors might know their losses well, while others have 
more difficulty observing and declaring these losses. These elements might need attention when 
designing the data collection instruments and sampling strategy.

4.2 SELECTION OF A SAMPLE
The selection of a sample of orchards or plots is a key component of a loss survey on fruits and 
vegetables. These guidelines recommend randomly selecting orchards and plots to ultimately 
ensure statistical representativeness of the results at the national and subnational levels.
If a country already conducts an agricultural survey that adequately covers fruits, permanent crops 
and vegetables, a loss assessment module can be appended to this survey, and the selection of 
the sample of orchards and plots simply follows the procedure adopted for the main survey. If 
crop-cutting or fruit-picking experiments are incorporated in the agricultural survey, typically to 
estimate production, it is likely that these experiments are performed on a subsample of the main 
selected sample, and no further selection is required. If not, for example because the production 
is based on farmers’ declarations, the additional step of selecting a subsample for physical 
measurement should be added to the sample design of the main survey (see Figure 10).
For countries that do not carry out agricultural sample surveys, a sample selection strategy is 
proposed for a stand-alone loss survey at the farm-level. This sample strategy can be applied in 
agricultural surveys in general. The proposed approach is based on a stratified multistage sampling 
based on the following steps:
  Stratification: The population of farms is first stratified to establish homogenous groups with 
respect to the variables of interests of the study, which in this case is mostly the production of 
the crop and losses. Variables such as agroecological zones (AEZ), regions, farm size, farm 
type (cultivation system) or land-use intensity for agriculture can be used for stratification. 
Regions/departments are typically used to allow for sub-national estimates, required for 
policy purposes, to be produced. Note that in countries with a complex topography, defining 
agroecological zones can be complicated. For example, in a country that has areas where 
fruits, such as apples, bananas, mangos and oranges are grown and thrive optimally, while 
in other areas, these crops may not benefit from the same growing conditions, their yields 
may be lower, and their percentage losses may be higher. In this example, agroecological 
zones are used as the stratification variable (see Figure 2).
  Determination of sampling units: The determination of the sampling units follows 
a multistage sampling design. To clarify, an example on fruit trees is presented. Other fruits 
and vegetables can follow a similar approach (with plots instead of orchards and vines/
sections of the plot instead of a fruit-bearing tree). The example is based on a three-stage 
sample selection, with districts 23 (or the corresponding administrative unit in the country) 
serving as primary sampling units (PSUs), villages or enumeration/census areas serve 
as secondary sampling units (SSUs) and orchards/plots (or holdings) serving as ultimate 
sampling units (USUs) for declaration-based estimates. For physical measurement, which 
is applied to a subsample, another sampling stage is added, where fruit-bearing trees are 
randomly selected as the USUs for the measurement. 24

stratum. Then, the proposed sampling design is to apply a probability proportional to size

This step of the survey consists of gathering the farmers’ own estimation of losses 28 with an 
interview on the different operations conducted on the farm: harvesting; immediate post-harvest 
operations, such as collection, pre-cooling, washing, sorting, grading, cleaning and packaging, 
transportation, and storage. These inquiries are intended to cover the entire sample of orchards. 
To conduct these interviews, a set of questionnaires were developed and field tested during a pilot

There are many ways to design the food loss module in a farm survey. The level of disaggregation of 
losses depends on the scope and number of questions allowed by the budget, available resources

the quantities handled and lost in each operation are collected one by one. Where this is not

vine, bush or on the ground usually reincorporated into the soil, while post-harvest losses are 
produce removed or spilled in any of the post-harvest operations. Harvest losses are not factored

to calculate loss percentages differs; therefore, while post-harvest losses use production as the 
denominator, harvest losses have to consider production quantities plus the unharvested amount. 
Moreover, harvest losses are at the boundary of SDG Subindicator 12.3.1a – Food Loss Index and 
they are singled out for reporting purposes.

Post-harvest loss:
All produce discarded during any post-harvest activity (pre-cooling, cleaning, sorting, grading, 
packaging, storage and transportation) is considered as post-harvest loss. This is independent of 
the actual cause or intention to discard the produce (in line with the simplification that losses are 
assigned to where the produce is removed from the supply chain, and not to where the damage 
was caused).
The decision to discard the produce stems from an evaluation of its quality and fitness for purposes. 
This is an area where qualitative and quantitative losses are more difficult to discern.
Questionnaire design for post-harvest operations: The two options are either one overall 
question that combines all post-harvest operations or separate questions for each operation (or 
groups of operation). Separate questions are preferable for capturing the dynamics on the farm 
and gathering more relevant information on the critical activities and relating them to the causes of 
losses. Moreover, it is usually easier for respondents to recall and provide information on specific loss 
points. In most cases, storage, transport and processing (if applied) are assessed separately from 
the other post-harvest operations because they happen in a different place and time, while other 
post-harvest operations are done immediately after harvest and may be covered with one question 
(collection, pre-cooling, cleaning, sorting, grading, packaging). Figure 10 presents a questionnaire 
example for post-harvest losses on the farm. A section for food loss in processing should be added

the farmer, such as coffee, olives and grapes (see also Chapter 3, Section 2 for guidance on how 
to formulate questions on processing losses).

Possible errors or biases
Farmer´s declaration-based estimates can be affected by biases and limitations, which may result 
in over or underestimation of the actual losses incurred on the farm. This can be the case when 
respondents do not know their actual losses, it is difficult for them to observe their losses, they do 
not want to disclose their real losses or bias their responses for any other reason. Possible errors 
or biases could be classified according to:
Interviewee bias: Data collection errors due to a lack of knowledge of the correct answer or 
deliberate falsification of data by tracing the original causal factor.
In most cases, farmers do not know their exact losses, as they tend to internalize losses in their 
production values.
Compared with other commodities, such as grains, tubers or roots, boundaries between pre-harvest 
and harvest are less marked in fruits and vegetables, which can lead to a misinterpretation of the 
questions or concepts and thus to declaration errors. For instance, in cases where some orchards 
are not harvested at all because of weather damage, farmers, when asked about their harvest 
losses, might include them although they are pre-harvest losses. Also, farmers might not consider 
the produce sent to composting as a loss and mistakenly not report them in the lost quantities.
In other cases, famers might also tend to respond more about economic losses instead of quantity 
losses, as income and prices can be a major concern. Therefore, it is essential that the questionnaires 
include clear food loss definitions and formulations of questions, and that these are well explained 
to the enumerators during the training.
Farmers might also introduce a deliberate bias in their declarations, either because they feel that 
having food losses has a negative connotation, or because they expect to receive government 
support in case they mention high harvest and post-harvest losses.
Recall bias: This bias occurs when respondents do not accurately recall losses, leading to 
incorrect estimates. One possible recall bias is the aggregation bias, for instance by aggregating 
all post-harvest operations or all production cycles. This can make it a challenge for the farmer to 
recall the losses and declare them properly, for instance when harvest periods for some crops are 
several weeks or months long and include multiple pickings. One way to overcome this issue is to 
further disaggregate the questions (for instance by operations or pickings). Another option is to ask 
for losses for a shorter period that the farmer can recall better, for example, of one average picking 
day (and calculate total food losses based on the number of picking days during the harvest period; 
this approach also assumes that loss percentages are relatively similar between pickings).
Some other factors can trigger important variations in the losses that further prevent farmers 
from declaring accurate loss estimates. The quantity of discarded fruits and vegetables varies 
depending on the destination and market situation, as quality standards are higher for the fresh

Reporting bias: This occurs when data is not reported accurately or completely, leading to

the various volumes needed to calculate loss percentages. These are total quantity harvested, 
quantities handled in post-harvest operations and loss quantities in the operations. Reporting

Interviewer becoming a variable: This occurs where the enumerator/interviewer/researcher, 
through their presence or way of applying the interview, might encourage a typical response. 
Interviewers play an important role in helping the farmer to respond when questions are complex, 
when the questions are formulated in technical terms or when the questions are too formal. However, 
it was observed that enumerators use their own words to explain the loss concept and, in some 
cases, rephrase the question on losses for the farmer. This can introduce biases, given that each 
enumerator will use their own examples and phrasings and they might influence the answers given.
Focus on technological versus socioeconomic factors: This involves too much focus on 
technological factors versus socioeconomic factors associated with post-harvest losses.

4.4 DATA COLLECTION WITH PHYSICAL MEASUREMENTS
Losses at harvest and during post-harvest operations can be measured with objective procedures 
inspired by field experiments in agriculture in a subsample of the orchards and plots chosen 
for the survey. If the produce requires multiple pickings, and the data collection based on 
declarations is repeated over several harvests/pickings, repeated measures should be carried out 
for physical measurements as well. The measurement protocols for each operation on the farm 
are described below.
Harvest of fruits and permanent crops:
After selecting the subsample of orchards and plots, the first step for the enumerator is to select 
the trees for the measurement.
Step 1: The enumerators must randomly select the fruit-bearing trees in each orchard and ensure 
that they are also ready to be harvested. 35 Before starting the measurements, the enumerator

should screen the selected trees or vines and check that their fruits or vegetables have reached 
the optimum maturity that enables harvesting. Trees or vines with immature fruits or vegetables 
would not be selected for harvesting and should be replaced in the subsample.
Step 2: Prior to harvesting, fruits and vegetables lying on the ground under the selected fruit-bearing 
trees or vines are removed. Since these losses occurred before the effective start of the harvest 
they are considered as pre-harvest losses.
Step 3: The farm worker harvests the fruit as usual to avoid introducing a bias in the result. The main 
risk here is that the farmer may apply a more efficient harvesting method or take extra care in 
the operation as they are being observed, which will result in lower or underestimated losses. 
A possible mitigation strategy is not to describe the purpose of the experiment in detail, but to 
remain intentionally vague.
Step 4: After harvesting, the ripe fruits left on the tree or vine or lying under the selected trees 
or vines should then be collected and weighed. The quantities lost during harvest consist of the 
quantity left on the plant plus the ones lying on the ground after harvest. Note that immature 
fruits or vegetables left on the tree or vine for subsequent reaping should not be counted as 
harvest losses.
Harvest of vegetable crops:
In each of the vegetable plots selected for physical measurement, one subplot is placed for the 
purpose of the experiment. If a country conducts crop-cutting experiments in the framework of its 
agricultural surveys, the procedure to select the subplots and physically measure and production 
and yields most likely already exists. In this case, it is recommended that the same procedure 
for the placement of the subplot be used for the loss assessment. The measurement is done in 
four steps:
Step 1: Designate a subplot within a plot. Subplots are typically squares of varying sizes depending 
on the crop under study and the planting density. In the case of vegetables, a subplot of 2 × 2 metres 
(4 m2) or of 1 m2 can be used. The placement of the subplot on the field is important. If, for example, 
it is systematically placed on a part of the field with a particularly high yield (e.g. benefiting from 
good solar exposure or drainage) or, conversely, in an area where yields are typically low (e.g. on the 
borders), the results will likely be distorted. For this reason, it is recommended that the placement 
of the subplot should always be made using a random procedure. A possible approach is given in 
GSARS (2018a).
Step 2: Clean the subplot. Before harvesting, all senescing vegetables lying on the ground in the 
selected area are removed. These losses occurred before the effective start of the harvest and are 
considered pre-harvest losses.
Step 3: This subplot is then harvested at the usual harvest time with the standard practice used 
by the farmer and the harvested quantities are measured. After harvesting, the total amount of 
harvested vegetables that remain on the field are collected and weighed separately.36 These are the 
harvest losses.

Step 4: Ensure that all the information to compile the harvest loss ratio has been collected. 
The harvest loss ratio is obtained by first calculating the adjusted production volume, which is 
equal to the harvested quantity plus the produce left on the field. Then, the volume of produce left 
on the field (i.e. the harvest losses) is divided by the adjusted production volume. This ratio gives 
the percentage food losses in harvesting.
Additional information may be needed to complement the physical measurement. For crops with 
multiple pickings or harvests, it is important to register the specific harvest during which the physical 
measurement took place because weather conditions can vary among them and affect loss levels 
(for instance, for crops with two harvest periods during a year, food losses may differ between the 
dry and rainy seasons). This information can help compare food loss from physical measurement 
(captured on the day of the visit) with those obtained from enquiry (usually captured for the whole 
year). For crops with multiple pickings, the picking can also influence the food loss percentage, as 
they may be comparably higher during later pickings (the last picking especially can be dedicated 
more to produce left on the plant or in a tree).
Post-harvest operations on-farm:
Post-harvest operations, such as the collection of a harvest, the grading and selection of fruits 
and vegetables, and the cleaning and packing operations may be done in one place and in a 
single operation, or in different places on the farm and in different periods. In the latter case, the 
protocol described below should be replicated for each operation. Each operation generates a 
certain quantity of discarded produce, eventually heaped on the floor, which may also contain other 
materials, such as stems and leaves. These are not to be considered for food losses; only the edible 
and non-edible parts of the produce are of relevance. Any produce that is removed (for any reason, 
whether damaged or because it does not meet the standards for export or national markets) is to 
be considered as a food loss. Measuring can be done in four steps:
Step 1: Select a sample of the produce. To determine the losses, a sample of fruits or vegetables 
(approximately 10 kg) can be drawn randomly from the produce brought to the grading and 
selection point on-farm. It is important to weigh and note the exact weight of the sample before 
starting the operations.37
Step 2: The farmer or worker then proceeds with the grading, cleaning and packaging operations 
as per usual practices. The farmer or worker should sort the produce into empty containers in 
the same grading categories they usually use (one container for each destination or category, 
e.g. primary market, secondary market and for disposal).
Step 3: Only the quantity of discarded produce is considered a food loss; the quantities 
for secondary destinations, for animal feed or industrial use are not to be counted as losses. 
The quantity to be disposed of should be weighed and the percentage of food loss should be 
calculated against the exact weight of the sample.
Additional information may be required for post-harvest operations as well. It may be relevant 
to ask the farmer about the destinations of the produce (by quality), and if the produce under 
observation was prepared for a specific market or represents the average. Information on the

On-farm storage losses:
To measure losses during storage on the farm, samples of produce must be taken at different points 
during a sufficiently long period to capture losses associated with, for example, pest infestations 
and moulds. The length of the period depends on the type of storage, on temperature and humidity, 
and on the products themselves: under cold storage conditions, for example, some fruits can be 
stored for as long as several weeks or months while fruits stored under ambient conditions typically 
do not last more than a week. For small or medium-size fruit producers in developing countries, a 
period of one or two weeks could be considered, with at least one visit at the start of storage and 
one at the end, to take samples of the produce and measure losses. The measurement can be done 
in three steps at each sample draw:
Step 1: Select a sample of produce. The selection process depends on how the produce is stored: 
(a) if the produce is stored in racks, boxes, cartons or bags, it can be subject to the random 
selection of one unit; (b) if the produce is stored in a heap, then a sample of, perhaps, 10 kg, can 
be selected from the heap. Ideally, the sample should be drawn for each side and level of the heap, 
as damaged produce may not be uniformly distributed. The 10 kg should be taken from three or 
four different places in the heap.
Step 2: Similar to before, the farmer or farm worker then proceeds with the grading and selection 
operation as per usual practices. They should separate the produce in empty containers in the 
same grading categories that they usually use (one container for each destination, for instance 
primary market or secondary market or to be discarded) and weigh them separately.
Step 3: Only the quantity of discarded produce is considered a food loss and the percentage 
of food loss should be calculated against the exact weight of the sample. The information on 
destinations provides insight on qualitative losses.
The last step is the calculation of percentage losses during the whole storage period. These are 
calculated as the difference between the relative losses measured at the end of the storage period 
(last sample draw) and at the beginning (first sample draw). It is critical to ask the grower for the 
average storage duration and condition of the crop. In the case of multiple pickings, an alternative 
method would be to compute an average of the percentage loss (eventually weighted by stored 
quantities on the day of the interview).

Estimating off-farm losses for fruits and vegetables
This chapter provides recommendations on how losses along the supply chain after leaving 
the farm-gate can be estimated at the national or subnational levels with acceptable statistical 
representativity. In these guidelines, they are referred to as off-farm losses. The same measurement 
methods can be applied for fruits and vegetables, as the loss points. Sampling procedures and 
assessment methods are similar. These methods are, therefore, presented jointly for these two 
groups of products.
5.1 OFF-FARM STAGES AND OPERATIONS
Off-farm supply chain stages in fruits and vegetables typically include different economic units 
as rural collection centres and packaging firms, processing industry, wholesalers, transportation 
companies and cold storage providers. In addition, intermediaries and traders are active in these 
off-farm stages, so they are included in the scope of the guidelines. Retail markets are considered 
part of SDG Subindicator 12.3.1.b – Food Waste Index, but some references are presented in cases 
in which data collection methods are similar, for example in wet markets or when wholesale and 
retail sales occur in the same marketplace. Since supply chains can differ considerably among 
commodities, countries and regions, it is recommended that a value chain analysis or desk study 
be conducted to map the value chain in order to set the most relevant stages and actors.
5.1.1 Generic supply chain stages
The two generic supply chains in Chapter 2, Section 1 represent simplified and aggregated value 
chain stages for standardization purposes in the context of the SDG Subindicator 12.3.1.a framework:
  Transport/storage/distribution: In the fruit and vegetable supply chain, this stage 
is mainly dominated by wholesalers, transportation and logistics firms, or providers; in some 
exceptional occasions cold storage service providers are active in this stage. Retailers 
can be included if they operate logistic systems comparable to wholesalers (the boundary 
between wholesaler and retailer is uncertain, for example, in wet markets). The economic 
units covered in this stage are characterized as providing services (storage, transportation 
and logistics) and distributing the produce without any direct value added to the produce.

There are a variety of transport and distribution stages in fruits and vegetables supply chains 
that should be specified and separated at the country level. For example, rural collection 
centres and traders38 can play an important role in some countries.
  Processing and packaging: Fruit and vegetable supply chains often operate through 
rural collection centres and packaging firms as the first off-farm stage where the produce 
is washed, pre-cooled, sorted, graded, stored, packed and distributed to various destinations. 
The packaging sector may apply minimal processing of fruits and vegetables, such as 
peeling, slicing, dicing and applying post-harvest treatments. 39 The packaging companies 
are distinguished from agro-processing companies that apply more sophisticated product 
development techniques. Common techniques for primary processing (i.e. a process that 
transforms the fresh produce into a less perishable food product) produce dry, dehydrated, 
fermented and frozen products, juices, sauces, jams, jellies and canned products. 
Packaging and processing units also trade, export and distribute their products, but as their 
main contribution to the supply chain is value addition, these units are distinguished from 
those involved in the transport/storage/distribution stage of fresh produce.
5.1.2 Definition of stages and stage boundaries
While these two generic stages may be relevant to standardize the supply chain among different 
commodities and commodity groups at the international level, fruit and vegetable supply chains 
are more likely to require a country-specific description in order to organize and structure data 
collection efforts. It is important to understand the sequence of stages the produce goes through 
from production to consumption. In many countries, different supply systems may coexist 
(for instance, export-oriented production and commercialization versus local production and 
distribution). A supply chain mapping or study helps in providing a country-specific description. 
The generic stages are described in greater detail below.

  Improved traceability identifies the origin of the produce and tracks it throughout the supply chain. This information can

first occurred.
  Increased efficiency highlights inefficiencies and bottlenecks in the system. This information can be used to optimize 
the supply chain and reduce costs, as well as tackling losses.
  Better risk management identifies potential risks in the supply chain, such as disruptions in the flow of goods or quality

  Improved communication between different stakeholders in the supply chain can help to ensure that everyone 
is on the same page and working towards the same goals.
  Increased transparency in the supply chain can help to build trust between different stakeholders and improve 
the overall sustainability of the supply chain.
  Better decision-making using information to improve the efficiency of the supply chain, reduce production and supply

Rural collection centres/packaging firms:
In general, rural collection centres and packaging firms are located close to the areas where fruits and 
vegetables are grown. Producers or intermediaries transport and sell the produce to the packaging 
firms, which then distribute the fresh produce to different markets. Some collection centres or 
packaging companies are operated by producer organizations/associations, others are private 
companies. In some cases, collection centres are owned by a single producer, hence, the producer 
operates in two stages of the supply chain: food losses up to the collection centre are considered 
on-farm if it only received produce from one farm, while the collection centre can be considered 
an off-farm stage if it receives fruits and vegetables from other growers. Collection centres and 
packaging firms distribute their produce to different markets; consequently, transportation losses 
are important to assess at this stage.
Processing companies/units:
This stage focuses on companies and economic units engaged in processing fruits and vegetables. 
Food losses cover only processed food that is obtained from a single primary input product, while 
processed food derived from a diverse range of commodities is considered part of food waste 
based on the considerations of boundaries presented in Section 2.3. Aggregated food losses 
along the supply chain require the computation in primary product equivalents at all stages. If a 
processing company produces frozen pizza, for instance, it is difficult and often of little relevance 
to derive a primary product equivalent to the tomato used to make the pizza. Food processors 
expressed their difficulty in breaking down their discards and estimating the losses for a single 
input product. Other reasons, which are not directly related to measurement, are that composite 
processed foods utilize processed input ingredients and additives that increase the shelf-life of 
products and replace home preparations.
A major challenge for measuring food loss is in the food processing industry, where the supply 
chains of different agricultural commodities cross and merge into a final combined product, and 
it is impossible for the industry to measure the separate amounts of food loss by agricultural 
product and compile the percentage losses. The more food is processed, the more difficult it is 
to disaggregate the data. Lessons from the pilot surveys suggested focusing on the first stage 
processing or single processed products that are directly derived from primary products, such as 
juice production, frozen or dried fruits and vegetables and marmalade. Products developed from a 
mix of commodities in which fruits and vegetables are only one of many inputs might not allow for 
assessing the removed quantities of single commodities, such as ready-made meals or frozen pizza 
that contain vegetables. Secondary processing may be excluded from a food loss assessment in 
some cases, as the produce is usually less perishable and does not have a distinguishable effect 
on national food loss levels. Discards from the food industry are treated as part of municipal and 
industrial waste.
Wholesalers (and retailers):
Wholesalers generally collect, commercialize, and distribute fruit and vegetable crops in large 
quantities to retailers, local markets, supermarkets, and eventually food processors and food 
services. Accordingly, wholesalers are essential in linking production with consumption. In most 
cases, wholesalers buy from farmers, rural collection centres, packaging firms, cooperatives, 
producers’ associations or intermediaries located in the production areas. Some wholesalers are at 
the same time large-scale producers, or cooperatives/producers’ associations that buy additional 
produce from other growers. In that case, the wholesaler should be treated separately based 
on its production activities (as a producer in on-farm food loss surveys), and as a wholesaler in 
off-farm surveys. In general terms, wholesalers do not transform the fruits or vegetables; they 
eventually engage in collection, transport and storage, and in grading and cleaning if needed.

In some countries, it is not possible to differentiate between wholesalers and retailers. For example, 
in cases in which wholesalers and retailers operate in the same market and provide food services, 
while at the same time they sell directly to consumers. On the other hand, some highly integrated 
retailers operate acquisition and distribution systems comparable to wholesalers.
Transportation, logistics and cold storage service providers:
In some fruits and vegetables supply chains, specialized transportation, logistics and cold storage 
providers are hired by packaging firms, marketing boards, wholesalers, exporters and supermarkets 
with and without chain stores. These companies are more common in large-scale distribution 
systems and export markets in which food safety and traceability regulations require specialized 
transport and storage facilities. In some countries and supply chains, transporters exist at the local, 
regional and national levels and utilize less specialized transportation services. The relevance of 
this stage for food loss measurement depends on how often external transport and storage service 
providers are hired, and if food losses are known to be relevant. In some countries, these service 
providers may use highly specialized equipment and record low food losses, in others, transport is 
a critical loss point for fruits and vegetables.
The structure and stages of fruits and vegetables supply chains can differ between crops, regions, 
countries and destination markets. Accordingly, mapping and analysing the supply chains at the 
commodity level before any data collection effort is a useful exercise to identify the actors in this 
stage of the supply chain (see Figure 11).

5.1.4 Stage operations
It is important to separate food supply stages from their respective operations and activities (stage 
operations). Food supply stages are intended to identify and group the actors or economic entities 
that fulfil a similar role in the supply chain. Stage operations are all activities the supply chain 
actors conduct related to the food. The operations are often very similar even between the off-farm 
stages. For example, storage and transport operations can take place both at the retail and the 
wholesale stages. Under the definitions of stages, losses that occur during a storage operation 
within one stage (for instance of a wholesaler), are not assigned to the storage stage but instead to 
the wholesaler stage, as the wholesalers conduct the operation. The same can apply to sorting and 
grading carried out by a packaging firm or by a wholesaler, for example.
For off-farm stages, operations are assigned to the economic unit that conducts the operation. 
This implies that within a stage, the companies and actors in that stage may not carry out the 
same operations. For instance, some packaging firms store the produce while others distribute it 
immediately after it has been packaged.
Losses during transport deserve special attention because transport links two stages of the supply 
chain. Transport can be hired as a service, or transporters can be intermediaries who buy and 
resell the product. In both cases, transport becomes a stage in the supply chain with its own data 
collection. Integrated transport, which is own transport carried out by the producer, processor or 
wholesaler, is assigned to the respective stages.
Quantitative losses during transport are the produce that is not handed over to the client. 
These losses can be due to rejection by the client, spillage, thefts, bad roads, road accidents, 
dysfunctional cooling systems, or any reason for the transporter not to reach the destination like 
highway closures, long waiting lines and delays at borders to be crossed to export produce as well 
as closed borders that cause the total loss of transported commodities. Other than these extreme 
cases, partially damaged produce during transport but handed over to the client is most probably 
measured during the grading process of the receiving entity. This can cause economic losses due 
to qualitative damage during transportation, but quantitative food losses will occur implicitly in the 
next stage, if the product is removed from the supply chain.
Where to measure and assign transportation losses before data collection must be decided and 
there must be neither gaps nor double reporting.

5.2 SAMPLE SELECTION IN OFF-FARM STAGES
An appropriate sampling design is required for each stage of the supply chain to select the units for 
the measurement of food whether by enquiries or physical measurements.
Off-farm stages of the supply chain are often not well covered in the national statistics system. 
Storage, transportation and processing companies are generally registered in business registers, 
which are used as sample frames for economic and industry surveys. However, as the registers 
cover all economic and industrial sectors, the food industry is in most cases characterized for the 
broad sector (“food” without further detail). In countries where the informal economy is common, 
business registers are incomplete. Small and medium size entities or operations located in rural 
areas may not appear in any business or administrative register. This can be critical for food loss 
measurement, as it is especially those rural economic entities that are often critical loss points 
where food losses may be high, and they may handle large shares of national food production. 
Accordingly, it may be necessary to create an ad hoc listing for the loss survey, either to focus on the 
target commodities and stages by disaggregating the existing registers, or to include unregistered 
entities with a targeted listing exercise. A sampling design for the off-farm assessment of fruits and 
vegetables is provided in Figure 12.
Rural collection centres and packaging firms:
Rural collection centres and packaging firms are mostly located close to the production areas. 
Only those operating in more integrated markets or supply export markets are registered in 
administrative or business registers. Rural collection centres, which are often run by producers or 
producer organizations, tend to be informal entities, making additional listing necessary. This would 
entail selecting districts randomly (PSU) and creating listings of all rural collection centres and 
packaging firms within the district. After that is completed, a representative number of centres and 
firms need to be selected randomly for data collection (USUs). When only a few units are present 
in a district, all the units in the district should be assessed.
Processing units:
Generally, there are only a limited number of entities involved in the processing industry in sampled 
enumeration areas (EAs) or districts (PSU). In some cases, the processing units in the sampled 
EAs or districts are located outside the bounds of the sampled areas (for instance, in urban areas). 
For such cases, a more direct approach should be adopted to select these units: either direct 
enumeration if the number is small enough or a simple random selection based on the listings 
of these units, which are usually available from the relevant country authorities and business 
registries. If cold storage or processing units are not available in the selected district, they may 
be selected from a nearby district or even outside the stratum, whichever is the closest. In some 
countries, food processing is conducted by small scale cottage industries or at the household 
level. To account for this, a stratified or separated sample design is useful to distinguish them from 
large processing entities. The listings of such units can often be obtained through household and 
employment surveys.
Transportation and cold storage services:
Similar to processing units, there is generally only a limited number of storage service entities, even 
at the state level. Most of them are located close to distribution centres and export routes. While the 
specialized transportation and logistics companies are registered in trading and business registers, 
these types of services that operate locally can often be identified through state-level listings.

Producer organizations, collection centres, packaging firms and wholesalers can help in completing 
the list of transportation service providers. Depending on the number of companies identified, 
a representative sample should be selected at the district levels, or, if insufficient, at the higher 
sampling stage.
Wholesalers (and retailers):
In each of the sampled districts, a small sample of wholesale (and retail) markets should be selected 
as SSUs, perhaps two of each. In each of these sampled markets, individual wholesalers and 
retailers should then be sampled as USUs. The size of the sample depends on the size of the 
markets, the coverage of the crop being investigated in these markets and the budget available for 
the study. In the strategy proposed in this document, two wholesalers and five retailers are selected 
in each type of market.
The process of selecting traders in each market can be complex given the multiplicity of actors and 
the changing nature and structure of these markets. In principle, the selection process should be 
random, based on a pre-determined list of traders in each market (or portion of it, if for example, 
the markets are organized by group of products). If the cost of establishing and maintaining such 
listings is too high, a more practical approach should be used to select traders using ad hoc or 
semi-random procedures, based, for example, on partial listings made in situ and on the spot. 
In some countries, wholesale and retail markets overlap. For example, when wholesalers access 
markets in the morning and retailers in the afternoon, these markets are closely connected and 
often face similar losses.
The loss assessment by enquiry should be carried out for each of the two selected wholesalers and 
the five retailers in the sampled markets. The physical measurements, however, are only performed 
for a randomly selected subset of these traders, for example two out of the five retailers and two 
wholesalers (i.e. all those selected for enquiry-based assessments).

5.3 DATA COLLECTION USING DECLARATIONS
As described earlier, food loss information on off-farm stages is collected for the core operations 
and activities conducted at each stage. Operations can be generalized as cleaning, sorting, grading, 
packaging, processing, storage and transportation. In this section, guidelines on data collection 
by enquiry are provided by operation for a generic questionnaire structure that can be used for 
any off-farm supply chain stage. Some additional stage-specific questions and information are 
highlighted for each of the stages. To summarize, the questionnaire is built in a modular way, by 
combining the sets of questions for each relevant operation. A final editing step will be needed to 
ascertain that there are no gaps, repetitions or inconsistencies in the final questionnaire for the 
respective stage.
Table 12 presents the different modules that comprise the questionnaire at off-farm stages. 
The seven questionnaire modules cover the main operations that are conducted in the stages off 
the farm. These operations are often repeatedly conducted along different stages, for instance 
reception/sorting/grading and transport is conducted by almost all off-farm stages. Sometimes rural 
collection centres, packaging companies and wholesalers conduct similar operations, but in different 
points of the value chain and thus with underlying different dynamics and characteristics. Minimal 
processing and trimming are usually linked to value added products (e.g. ready-for-consumption) 
and applied by packaging and trading companies, and only sometimes by rural collection centres 
or wholesalers. These latter tend to be more concentrated on the distribution of the produce from 
the rural areas to the consumption zones. While major processing is characteristic for processors 
only and is a module less relevant for other stages.
A reduced set of modules will be applied to the transport and storage service providers. These only 
receive and take the produce in custodianship for the service of transportation or storage. In turn, 
the modules on transportation and storage can be further detailed (e.g. more detailed information 
on the infrastructure, equipment, duration of the transport or storage, number of trips and turnovers, 
among others). With local or informal transport services, it was observed that transporters also sort 
the produce before handing it over, removing damaged produce.
The final questionnaire by stage can be made of several modules.

MODULE 6: TRANSPORTATION LOSSES
As mentioned above, produce that is damaged during transportation but is not discarded by the 
transporter, either during or at the end of the transport stage and before being handed over to the 
next actor, should not be recorded as a transport loss. It should be recorded as a loss in the stage

stage during grading and cleaning. This avoids double counting.

losses are related to incidents, such as accidents, closed highways, cold system failure or others.

on how much produce was damaged while being transported could be included in a questionnaire 
about transportation losses.

Complementary modules: Qualitative and economic losses:
Qualitative research approaches have been successfully used to investigate food waste at the later 
stages of the food supply chain − in retail (Mohammed et al., 2018) and consumption (Graham-Rowe, 
Jessop and Sparks, 2014). However, it has rarely been applied to look at food losses in primary 
production, particularly regarding farmers’ attitudes to food loss. Since they will have the most 
impact in reducing losses in primary production, it is important to understand their perspective on 
food loss and waste.

Qualitative and related economic losses as defined in Section 2.2 are an important aspect 
of the fruits and vegetables supply chains; moreover, qualitative and economic losses are part 
of the broader conceptual definition of food losses, although they are not in the scope of SDG 
Subindicator 12.3.1.a – Food Loss Index. In some countries, these can be quite relevant, thus their 
measurement can be added to the quantitative measurement of food losses.
Economic losses occur when the produce was meant for a certain market but was redirected 
to another destination where the product has a lower economic value or it was discarded. This 
happens for instance when the crop does not meet quality requirements or is partially damaged. 
However, economic losses or lower prices can be due to low demand or limited access to markets, 
which are not connected to any qualitative considerations. While the broader concept of qualitative 
losses includes the reduction of economic value, it also refers to the reduction of nutritional value, 
food safety and/or consumer appreciation. These qualitative losses do not always translate into an 
economic loss.
The challenge with assessing qualitative and economic losses and their impact is three-fold. First, 
a reduction in the quality can cause the products to be removed from the human food supply chain 
and to be diverted to secondary utilizations but the product still remains in the food system, for 
instance for animal feed. Second, a reduction in the quality may result in a reduction in the price, 
which ultimately causes an economic loss. However, it is difficult to ascertain if a decrease in the 
economic value of a product is due specifically to a decrease in quality or to market dynamics. 
Third, the quality standards differ by country. Some countries would consider a certain quality 
unacceptable and discard it while in others the food would still be consumed irrespective of 
bruising or a decrease in nutrients. These characteristics are difficult to capture in a consistent 
manner across countries.
Depending on the country context and crop, different options can be tested to collect qualitative 
losses (Mohammed et al., 2018). These can be added to the core quantitative measures. Delgado, 
Schuster and Torero (2017) summarized and tested the following approaches based on declarations:
  A self-reported method uses direct questions are asked about the qualitative losses. 
The questions focus on the total quantity that has been damaged, visible quality deterioration 
and re-direction of lower quality and of prices destinations.
  A price method is based on the assumption that higher (lower) values of a commodity reflect 
higher (lower) quality. A decrease in price, all else being equal, is a proxy for lower quality.
  An attribute method is based on inferior visual, tactile and olfactory product characteristics 
declared by the respondent. These attributes are identified prior to the survey implementation 
in collaboration with commodity experts, local experts and value chain actors.
  A category method is based on quality categories. Each product is categorized according 
to its end use and each category is associated with a crop damage coefficient, indicating 
the percentage of the crop that is damaged within each category.

5.4 DATA COLLECTION USING PHYSICAL MEASUREMENTS
The measurement protocols for physical measurements along the supply chain are very similar to 
those presented for the on-farm assessment of fruits and vegetables for the various operations 
(basic processing activities, storage and transportation) and for the different actors of the supply 
chain (wholesalers, retailers, cold storage units and processing units). Accordingly, please refer 
to Chapter 4, Section 4 for a detailed presentation of physical measurements of losses during 
collection, sorting, grading, cleaning and storing of the produce.
The following paragraphs delve into the difficulties in carrying out physical measurements during 
transportation and processing, as they entail some additional complex elements that hinder the 
implementation of a sample survey.
Transportation losses
As transportation losses refer to produce not handed over to the destination point, physical 
measurement of the quantity discarded is usually not practical and, therefore, the collection of 
loss data is mostly through declaration. Load tracking 43 is an objective measurement technique 
to measure losses along the supply chain that can be applied to some extent in one stage of 
the supply chain only. It is an accurate way to measure losses in this instance transport loss is 
measured by sampling a representative quantity of produce in the load depending on the size of 
the load. For a full truck load, it would sample from a representative number of bags 44 at the top, 
middle and bottom of the load, etc. The method consists of the following key elements that need 
to be considered in design and implementation.
a. Choosing the “load”
A load is a certain quantity of product that can be followed (tracked) on its way through (part of) 
the food supply chain as one batch, and the changes in weight and quality of the load can be 
measured or assessed to estimate levels of losses. Loads can vary widely in size, weight and 
volume, depending on the crop and the packaging, transport and storage practices. A load can 
consist of a truck load of produce that will be monitored from the moment it is loaded on to the 
transportation vehicle to the point where it is unloaded and handed over to the next actor. If a 
considered load comes from only one farmer, or a few farmers, then the farmer/farmers must be 
chosen with consideration being given to their practices being similar to those of most producers 
in the selected food supply chain.
b. Unit of measurement or experimental unit
These are units taken (sampled) from a load. The most practical is to make use of the units that are 
being generated and used in the food supply chain, for example a bunch of bananas or a crate of 
tomatoes. In addition to their measurement, a decision must be made as to how many samples will 
be taken from the unit of measure. It depends on the size of such a unit, whether the whole unit will 
be evaluated and measured, or whether a representative smaller sample will be taken from the unit. 
This latter unit will normally be a weight or volume unit kilograms and tonnes, for example.
43 The load tracking method relies on evaluating the weight and/or quality of (a sample of) a load of food product as 
it moves through a supply chain.
44 Sacks, crates or any other packaging used.

c. Sampling
In order to estimate the food losses incurred during transportation, samples must be taken at a 
minimum of twice for a load; once when the load enters the transportation stage, and once when it 
exits the stage. For example, taking samples of mangos when they are being loaded on the truck 
and taking samples when they are being unloaded.
In all cases, the weight or volume of the unit and the samples has to be measured. Based on the 
samples, the total weight as well as the quality of the load can be determined.
Often a two-stage sampling is required: 1) a systematic selection of units from the load (top, middle 
and bottom of the load); and 2) a random sample from the selected units to be a measurable unit. 
For example, from a bunch of bananas (first stage sample) a few bananas (second stage sample) 
can be picked randomly. In all cases, the weight or volume of the unit and of the samples has to 
be measured.
d. Tracking
The sampling, as described by point c on sampling, has to be applied twice for a load: once when 
the load enters the transportation stage, and again when it exits the stage. In this way the food 
losses incurred during the event can be measured.
The enumerators must carefully mark the load(s) and follow it(them) to the destination to collect 
data on weight changes and/or quality changes upon arrival. However, the enumerators will have 
to consider the reality of the food supply chain; it is sometimes impossible to prevent the load from 
being mixed with other loads. Food supply chain actors may be observed doing it either by mistake 
or on purpose.
e. Quantitative losses
At each sample draw, the discarded produce and the retained produce should be segregated and 
weighed separately. Quantitative losses are, therefore, equal to the weight of food discarded that 
are measured in a load tracking activity. The quantification of lost weight by estimating it before 
and after a food supply chain activity is more complicated when the nature of the activity leads 
to a reduction of weight, for example drying. In these cases, one should instead determine the 
percentage of the product that has become unfit or less fit for processing, sales or consumption 
and will be considered lost.
For fruits and vegetables, the issues relate to: compression and abrasion damage due to improper 
packaging of the produce; poor packing of the produce in the packaging container; and poor 
loading and unloading of the produce. Weight loss of the produce, e.g. leafy vegetables that have a 
high surface-to-volume ratio, may weigh less due to water loss if not transported in a cool vehicle 
or cooled using a damp cloth.
f. Qualitative losses
To measure qualitative losses, especially the produce that was damaged during transportation 
(not counted as a quantitative transport loss), the method used for storage losses can be applied: a 
sample of produce is selected just after the produce is loaded into the vehicle. The sample selection 
method depends on how the produce is transported, whether in loose form or in bags or crates as

described earlier. The same process is repeated at the time of unloading. At each sample draw, the 
damaged and undamaged fruit should be segregated, weighed separately and, where necessary, 
the moisture content should be recorded. The damage incurred during transport is measured 
based on the weight difference between the produce damaged at loading and unloading, adjusted 
for changes in moisture content45 where necessary. Different moisture and threshold may apply to 
different crops (leafy vegetables, thick skinned produce, waxed produce, etc.). These are qualitative 
aspects that go beyond the scope of these guidelines and that should be defined elsewhere.
Processing losses
In the case of primary processing to transform fresh produce into jams, marmalades, and juices or 
into frozen, canned or dried products, processing losses can be measured with the method applied 
for grading. A sample of produce should be selected and tracked throughout the processing. 
The produce removed from the processing must be collected and weighed. When the removed 
produce is semi-processed or processed, technical conversion factors are required to convert the 
removed product to the same status as the initial sampled quantity.
The produce can be discarded in three main stages of the processing: 1) when sorting 
input ingredients; 2) during processing; and 3) during quality control of the output product. 
The measurement procedure must be conducted at every point of the processing process 
where the produce is removed. From the produce is removed, the operators should separate 
produce being reused from produced being discarded. Only discarded produce is food loss in 
this situation.
The physical measurement of processing losses is also challenging as the enumerators may not 
have access to the processing facilities for a number of reasons including food safety regulations. 
The measurement requires the active collaboration of the processors, which is hard to achieve in 
a sample survey. This kind of tracking of a commodity is more feasible in a case study. Moreover, 
the processing stage is relatively less important for fruits and vegetables that are mostly handled 
as fresh produce, and in many countries the processing sector is concentrated in few businesses. 
A case study of the fruits and vegetables processing sector may be the appropriate alternative.

Conclusion
These guidelines are intended to provide practical instructions to statistically sound approaches 
to estimate losses in fruits and vegetables both on the farm and along the supply chain (off the 
farm). The following aspects are elaborated in the guidelines: the definition of losses for fruits and 
vegetables and the boundaries between pre-harvest harvest and post-harvest; the sample design 
and selection procedures; questionnaire design with minimum information sets and recommended 
questions for each activity and stage of the supply chain; the operational approaches to capture 
losses at the farm or supply-chain levels using declarative and physical measurements; and the 
estimation procedure to extrapolate results at the required geographical or administrative level.
This document aims to fill a gap by providing practical guidance to implement a statistically sound 
approach to measure harvest and post-harvest losses for fruits and vegetables at the country level 
and along the whole supply chain. The guidelines strived to consolidate the loss measurement 
aspects into a single body of text. Presenting the different components of food measurement 
(concepts, sampling, data collection options, representativeness) in a comprehensive consistent 
and holistic manner rather than in isolation in separate documents is a major value-added aspect 
of this document.
The guidelines provide detailed recommendations on how to carry out each step of the assessments. 
It should be noted, however, that guidance is provided with overall approach and should not be 
treated as a unique solution that is applicable to any situation or country. The suggested procedures 
must be adapted to the sectoral and institutional setting where the assessment is being conducted, 
the specific objective of the assessment (e.g. focus on certain crops or specific loss points), the 
requirements in terms of accuracy and representativity (e.g. only national-level vs subnational level), 
the capacity constraints and the resources available to carry out the measurements.
Finally, this publication is a living document that will be updated as more countries will begin to 
use similar approaches to measure losses and as new insights are gained on the relevance and 
applicability of the proposed data collection and estimation procedures, and as new solutions 
emerge that can generate good quality results.

Glossary of statistical terms
Estimate: A particular value that the estimator takes for a given sample.
Non-sampling error: Errors other than sampling errors, such as those resulting from non-response 
or incompleteness and inaccuracy of response or measurement.
Parameter: Any function of the values of the population units. Suppose a population consists 
of the  distinct and identifiable units  and let  be the value of the variable , the 
characteristic under study, for the  unit , . The unit may be a parcel and the 
characteristic under study may be the area under a particular crop, for example. Some of the most 
common parameters estimated in surveys are population totals ( , e.g. total area under 
a particular crop) and population mean ( , e.g. average parcel area under a particular crop).
Percentage standard error (or coefficient of variation): The percentage standard error of the 
estimator  is defined as , where  denotes the standard error.
Population (or universe): A collection of all units of a specified type as per the objective of the 
survey at a particular point or period of time. For example, the set of parcels of land, households, 
farmers or orchards in a region can be considered a population.
Questionnaire: The support, either in paper or electronic form, used by the enumerators for 
recording the data. Its design is an important element for meeting the objective of the study.
Random sample: A sample constituted by units selected using a random procedure. Each unit in 
the population has a predetermined probability of selection.
Sample: A subset of the population, with a known size defined as the number of distinct units of 
the sample.
Sampling: A process of selecting a subset of units from the population.
Sampling error: An error in the estimation of a parameter resulting from the estimation being based 
on a subset (sample) of the population.
Simple random sampling without replacement: In this method, the units are drawn one by one, 
with equal probability of selection assigned to each unit at each draw. The unit selected at any 
particular draw is not replaced in the population before the next draw.

Agriculture The practice of cultivating the soil, growing crops, or raising livestock for 
human use, including the production of food, feed, fibre, fuel or other 
useful products.
Agroecology The application of ecological principles to agriculture systems and 
practices.
Agroforestry A land use management system in which trees or shrubs are grown 
around or among crops or pastureland.
Compost A mixture of ingredients used as plant fertilizer to improve the soil’s 
physical, chemical and biological properties.
Conversional 
agriculture
A farming system that uses synthetic chemicals and fertilizer to maximize 
the yield of a particular crop.
Crop residues Materials left on cultivated land after the crop has been harvested.
Farmyard manure A mixture of dung, urine, litter and leftover materials from roughages 
and fodder fed to animals.
Integrated 
soil fertility 
management
The enhancement of crop productivity while maximizing the agronomic 
efficiency of applied inputs.
Nutrient elements The elements required for the normal life cycle of an organism whose 
function cannot be substituted by any other chemical compound.
Organic farming An agricultural system that uses organic fertilizers such as compost.
Organic fertilizer Fertilizer consisting of degradable materials derived from plant and 
animal products.
Organic matter A reservoir of nutrients that can be released to the soil.
Plant nutrients The chemical elements that are essential to the nourishment of plant 
health.
Recycling The process of converting waste materials into new reusable materials 
and objects.
Soil The upper layer of earth in which plants grow.
Soil fertility The ability of soil to sustain agricultural plant growth.
Soil health The capacity of soil to function as a living system to sustain plant and 
animal productivity.
Soil structure The way individual particles of sand, silt and clay are assembled.
Soil texture The proportion of sand, silt and clay-sized particles that make up the 
mineral fraction of the soil.
Synthetic fertilizer Chemically manufactured materials containing one or more of the 
primary nutrients necessary for plant growth.

CHAPTER 1  
Introduction
About integrated soil fertility 
management
Soil fertility is the ability of soil to sustain 
plant growth and optimize crop yield. Current 
advocated approaches and techniques target 
enhancing soil fertility and crop production while 
minimizing the environmental impact. Therefore, 
advancing food security and environmental 
sustainability in farming systems requires an 
integrated soil fertility management (ISFM) 
approach that maximizes crop production 
while minimizing the mining of soil nutrient 
reserves and the degradation of the physical 
and chemical properties of soil that can lead 
to land degradation, including soil erosion. 
These soil fertility management practices most 
often include the use of organic inputs, crop 
rotation with legumes and the use of improved 
germplasm, combined with the knowledge on 
how to adapt these practices to local conditions.
ISFM aims at maximizing the efficiency of the 
agronomic use of nutrients and improving 
crop productivity. This can be achieved by 
intercropping, using cover crops, practising crop 
rotation, adding farmyard manure or compost, 
using green manure, and other processes that 
minimize losses through leaching below the crop 
rooting zone by improved water and nutrient 
application.
The ISFM approach can be applied to both 
agricultural and non-agricultural settings, such 
as home gardens. ISFM takes advantage of all 
appropriate ISFM options including, but not 
limited to, soil and water conservation practices, 
crop residue management, recycling of organic 
wastes, the implementation of agroforestry 
practices related to ISFM, and the preparation 
and use of fertilizers.
Why integrated soil fertility management? 
It’s easy to question the need for ISFM when 
inorganic fertilizers alone very often succeed 
at boosting plant growth and crop productivity. 
The United Republic of Tanzania’s soils are 
inherently of poor quality, and the current land 
degradation has greatly amplified biodiversity 
loss, altered species abundance, worsened 
climate impact, and reduced ecosystem 
services. This is primarily due to inadequate soil 
fertility and poor management, undermining 
productivity. Considering the expense of 
fertilizers for small-scale farmers and their 
limited use, it is recommended to adopt ISFM, 
a strategy that combines fertilizer with other 
soil management methods tailored to local 
conditions to enhance crop productivity.1 
The application of soil fertility management 
practices should be applied, and the knowledge 
to adapt them to local conditions maximizes 
fertilizer and organic resource use efficiency and 
crop productivity. These practices necessarily 
include appropriate fertilizer and organic input 
management combined with the utilization 
of improved germplasm. Good agronomic 
practices in terms of planting dates, plant 
densities and weeding are essential for ensuring 
the efficient use of scarce nutrients.
Productivity is substantially enhanced when 
IFSM is successfully adopted. What’s more, a 
positive synergistic effect between organic and 
inorganic inputs is often observed. As a result, 
the efficiency of rainfall use is greatly enhanced. 
ISFM targets nutrient resources within crop 
rotation cycles, preferably by including legumes, 
thus going beyond recommendations for a 
single crop.2
Purpose and rationale for the 
training guide 
The purpose of the training guide is to provide 
extension officers and farmers with an overall 
understanding of ISFM concepts as well as 
the principles for implementing them at the 
field level. The participants are guided on the

The Guide aims to equip small-scale farmers, 
agricultural extension officers and other 
facilitators with knowledge and skills on how to 
adopt and implement ISFM in order to enhance 
their capacity to make the right decisions

How was the training guide prepared?
The Guide was prepared through rigorous 
efforts of individuals from different 
organizations. It therefore draws from a wealth 
of experience gathered by these individuals 
through their long time of service to farmers 
in different parts of the United Republic of 
Tanzania. The Guide also benefits from research 
studies by authors engaged in agroecology 
and by different universities and organizations 
that have implemented ISFM, and from 
lessons learned. Content adopted from other 
research has been duly acknowledged. This 
Guide will be continuously improved based 
on the users’ practices, improvements and 
technological advancements, and will support 
the transformation towards healthy soils and 
healthy food.
How is the Guide organized? 
The first part of this manual introduces the 
intention for developing the Guide. It provides 
details about the intended users of the manual 
and its purpose. Subsequently, the next parts 
of this manual provide an understanding of 
the ISFM principles and practices, as well as its 
establishment and implementation.
The benefits of using this Guide
Farmers and extension agents will benefit from 
using this Guide as a comprehensive reference 
tool to make informed decisions, tailor strategies 
to their specific contexts, and implement 
sustainable and environmentally friendly soil 
fertility management practices.
The principles of integrated soil 
fertility management
ISFM is an approach that aims to enhance 
soil fertility and agricultural productivity in a 
sustainable and balanced way.3 The principles of 
ISFM are as follows:
1. Balancing nutrients: ISFM focuses on 
balancing a combination of essential 
nutrients in the soil by using organic 
materials such as compost, crop residues 
and manure, together with mineral fertilizers, 
to ensure that all required nutrients are 
available to plants.
2. Site-specific management: The approach 
recognizes that different soils and agro-
ecological conditions require tailored 
solutions by adapting practices to the specific 
needs and conditions of each field or plot.
3. Crop rotation and diversity: Rotating 
different crops in a sequence helps prevent 
nutrient depletion and pest build-up by 
planting a diverse range of crops to improve 
soil health and reduce the risk of disease 
outbreaks. 
4. Minimizing soil disturbance: Reducing 
excessive tillage and other practices that 
disturb the soil structure helps maintain its 
fertility, prevent erosion, and enhance water 
retention.
5. Conservation agriculture: ISFM promotes 
the practices of minimum tillage, cover 
cropping and maintenance of crop residues 
on the field to protect the soil from erosion, 
maintain moisture, and improve organic 
matter content.
6. Use of organic matter: Incorporating 
organic materials such as compost, crop 
residues and manure into the soil enhances

7. Sustainable nutrient management: ISFM

8. Local resources utilization: Using locally

SESSION I 
Soil as a living organism
Why is soil considered a living 
organism?
• Soil is the land surface layer that supports 
plant growth.
• Like a living organism, soil breathes and 
requires air and water to sustain life.
• Good soil hosts a large biomass of 
microorganisms essential for decomposing 
organic matter and releasing nutrients for 
plant growth.
• Soil is home to macro-organisms like 
earthworms, insects and mites, as well 
as other small invertebrates, which aid in 
aeration, organic matter breakdown and soil 
structure formation (Figure 1).
• Soil organisms cycle essential nutrients such 
as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium, 
transforming them into forms accessible to 
plants.
• Soil organisms respire, releasing carbon 
dioxide and influencing the global carbon 
cycle.
• Soil organisms are vital for ecosystem 
functions such as plant growth, water 
filtration and carbon storage, linking 
soil health to the health of aboveground 
ecosystems. 
Introduction to soil health 
management
Soil health is the capacity of soil to function 
as a living system, with ecosystem and land 
use boundaries, to sustain plant and animal 
productivity, maintain or enhance water and air 
quality, and promote plant and animal health. 
Healthy soils maintain a diverse community of 
soil organisms that help to control plant disease, 
insect and weed pests; form beneficial symbiotic 
associations with plant roots; recycle essential 
plant nutrients; improve soil structure with 
positive repercussions for soil water and nutrient 
holding capacity; and ultimately improve crop 
production.
Soil fertility and its importance
Soil fertility is the capacity of soil to retain, cycle 
and supply essential nutrients for optimal plant 
growth. Fertile soil can supply all essential plant 
nutrients, i.e. nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, 
calcium, magnesium, sulphur, iron, zinc, copper, 
boron, molybdenum and nickel, in amounts 
needed to produce yields with high quantity 
and quality. Sources of soil nutrients include soil 
minerals, soil organic matter and biological 
nitrogen fixation.
Factors that negatively affect soil fertility
• inappropriate irrigation methods;
• excessive irrigation;
• natural disaster, i.e. floods and windstorms;

Soil fertility evaluation techniques and soil 
texture determination 
Soil fertility status can be evaluated through 
qualitative and quantitative assessments by 
using the following methods: 
• the biological method (pot culture, 
Neubauer seedling method); 
• the visual method, consisting of observing 
the symptoms of nutrient deficiency, such as 
yellowing leaves (nitrogen deficiency), purple 
edges (phosphorus deficiency) and pale 
leaves (potassium deficiency); 
• the plant analysis method, consisting of 
examining the crops’ appearance; and
• the soil analysis method, consisting in 
digging two small holes in different spots 
and comparing soil textures, colour and 
structure; sandy soils lack nutrients, while 
clay soils hold too much water. 
Integrate these simple methods with organic 
matter, crop residue and manure to enhance 
soil fertility. This hands-on approach saves time 
and money, and will provide an overview of the 
ability of the soil to supply essential nutrients for 
optimum plant growth and development.
Assessing soil fertility involves evaluating the 
soil’s capacity to support plant growth by 
providing essential nutrients and favourable 
conditions. FAO uses various methods to assess 
soil fertility, such as analysing soil samples in 
laboratories, conducting on-site evaluations, 
and employing remote sensing techniques.
Laboratory soil analysis is carried out by 
collecting soil samples from the field to be sent 
to laboratory service providers (e.g. Sokoine 
University of Agriculture – Soil Science; Tanzania 
Agricultural Research Institutes (Mlingano and 
Selian) - Soil Service; Nelson Mandela African 
Institution of Science and Technology in Arusha, 
and International Institute of Tropical Agriculture 
in Dar es Salaam), or by using soil testing kits 
from the nearest agricultural extension service 
followed by the interpretation of results.
Soil texture can be determined using the soil 
texture triangle (Figure 3) and the proportions of 
clay, silt and sand in a sample. Understanding 
the soil texture provides information about 
factors such as the permeability and water-
holding capacity of a given soil. These qualities 
impact the types of plants that can grow as well 
as erosion rates.
The proportions of particle types are found 
by suspending a sample in water (put soil 
and water in a jar and shake it), allowing the

ISFM and its importance
ISFM involves maintaining and enhancing soil 
nutrient content, structure and health for optimal 
plant growth. It ensures balanced nutrient 
levels, supports robust crops, and prevents 
environmental harm. By recycling nutrients, it 
promotes sustainability and resilience to climate 
change while reducing production costs and 
preventing soil degradation. 
Customized practices, including organic matter 
application, controlled fertilizer use and various 
agricultural techniques, ensure context-specific 
success. Adopting these practices safeguards 
agricultural productivity and the environment for 
future generations.
ISFM and implications for sustainable 
farming
In conventional farming, ISFM includes 
appropriate inorganic and organic fertilizer 
resource management combined with the 
utilization of improved crop varieties and 
adapting to local conditions. This results in 
interactive specific management practices and 
investment choices, which improves farmers’ 
decisions concerning weed management, the 
choice of timing and spacing of fertilizer use, 
and crop variety. The outcome is improved 
productivity, enhanced soil quality and a more 
sustainable farming system.

SESSION II 
Integrated soil fertility management 
practices
Soil and water conservation practices
Soil and water conservation practices focus on 
the control of water movement and improved 
vegetation cover of the soil to reduce water 
runoff on the soil surface.
The use of cover crops such as large canopy 
crops and runner crops, the planting of large 
dense pasture around the field, and the 
application of organic mulches (e.g. dry leaves, 
rice husks, eggshells) and inorganic mulches 
such as plastic sheets protect the soil from 
erosion and evapotranspiration by reducing 
water runoff while conserving moisture and 
improving soil fertility upon decomposition.
Combining cover crops with physical rainwater 
harvesting techniques, such as half-moon 
micro-catchment, trenches, cut-off drains and 
contours, helps to protect soil against soil 
erosion (Figure 4). 
Conservation tillage
This practice involves growing crops from year to 
year with zero or minimum soil disturbance.
The practice includes no-till, strip-till, ridge-
till and mulch-till systems (Figure 5). When 
compared to conventional practices, a minimum 
tillage system can maintain plant residue by 
more than 40 percent while reducing the volume 
of soil disturbed, thus preserving the soil surface 
for environmental and economic viability.
Crop residue management in the field
Crop residues contain a good proportion of 
essential nutrients exported by plants from 
the soil. When left to decompose in the field, 
crop residues increase soil organic matter and 
enhance nutrient cycling and retention.

Agroforestry practices in ISFM
Agroforestry is a regenerative farming practice 
defined as an intentional combination of 
trees with crops and/or livestock into a single, 
integrated system. Commonly, these practices 
are used for managing soil fertility.
Utilizing trees in your cropping system through 
agroforestry can be a tool for building healthy 
and fertile soils.

Agroforestry can take many forms: rotating 
animals through an orchard; alley-cropping 
of crops between rows of fruit or timber trees; 
planting rows of windbreaks along the edges 
of the farm; integrating livestock with trees; 
establishing multi-layered forest gardens; and 
practising silvopastoral systems (Figure 8). 
Despite the agroforestry system, it incorporates 
trees that improve soil fertility by increasing 
organic matter, boosting microbiological activity, 
mineralizing nutrients into a more plant-
available form, cycling nutrients up from deep 
soil layers to shallow ones, fixing nitrogen (in 
the case of leguminous trees) and preventing 
nutrient losses due to leaching, erosion and 
runoff.
Use of organic fertilizers
Organic fertilizers are degradable materials 
derived from plant and animal products brought 
by the farmer to the crop field as fertilizer 
resources to replenish soil nutrients. These 
include farmyard manure (Figure 9a), green 
manure, composts (Figure 9b), poultry manure, 
manure tea and plant tea.
Organic fertilizers improve the soil’s physical 
properties. They also increase humus content, 
change the biological properties of soil, and 
help in the flourishing of beneficial macro- and 
microorganisms.
Organic amendments increase soil carbon and 
nitrogen content, which results in enhanced soil 
fertility and crop productivity, and are also eco-
friendly and cost-effective.

• Solid organic fertilizers, e.g. compost, green
manure, farmyard manure

Benefits of organic fertilizers

.

• They increase the amount of organic matter
in the soil.
• They improve soil air circulation and water
holding capacity.
• They provide food for soil microorganisms
and increase biodiversity
, hence soil health.
• They improve soil structure.
Use of inorganic fertilizer
Inorganic fertilizer is one of the components 
of ISFM, providing essential nutrients (e.g
. 
nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium) in a 
readily available form to plants. Their use is 
balanced with organic amendments, improved 
crop varieties, conservation practices, efficient 
resource management, and local knowledge to 
create a sustainable and productive agricultural 
system.
The inappropriate use (excessive amount, wrong 
choice/source/time of application) of inorganic 
fertilizer can result in a change of soil pH, which 
has negative impacts on the soil ecosystem, 
resulting in poor soil quality. When used as 
a single source, inorganic fertilizers tend to 
accelerate the decomposition of soil organic 
matter, which leads to the degradation of soil 
structure, especially in soils constrained by 
smaller quantities of organic matter.
Integrating the minimum inorganic fertilizer 
with the best sustainable agronomic practices 
(e.g. soil and water conservation practices, use 
of animal manure or compost, crop residue 
management, suitable agroforestry practice) 
improves soil health while increasing soil 
productivity.

Preparation of compost
Compost is a mixture of ingredients used as 
plant fertilizer and to improve soil’s physical, 
chemical and biological properties. It is 
commonly prepared by decomposing plants, 
food waste, recycling organic materials and 
manure.
The factors to consider during the 
preparation of compost are:
1. Moisture content – The recommended
moisture content of compost ranges between
40 percent and 60 percent: if too high or
too low, this will affect aerobics microbial
activities. The moisture content could be
measured using a compost moisture meter,
or by a visual inspection, or by touching
the compost. If the pile is too dry, it will lack
heat and there’ll be little evidence of organic
material breakdown. If the compost is too
wet, it appears slimy and smells bad.
2. T
emperature – Temperature is vital
in compost preparation, affecting
microorganism activity. The ideal range is
between 49°C (120°F) and 71°C (160°F).
Composting has two phases: the mesophilic
phase (20–45°C) and the thermophilic
phase (45–70°C), where different bacteria
break down material. High temperatures,
55–70°C, destroy pathogens and
weed seeds. Monitoring and adjusting
temperature, aeration and moisture ensures
effective composting.
3. Degree of acidity – The degree of acidity
of the material used impacts the entire
composting process: an acidity level of the
organic materials that is too low inhibits
carbon dioxide and heat generation; and
an acidity level that is too high can cause
excessive volatilization of ammonia, leading 
to nitrogen loss. T
urning or aerating your 
compost to improve air circulation and foster 
aerobic bacteria is the best way to reduce 
acidity. Also, make sure that there is plenty of 
brown plant material in the compost. Adding 
wood ash to compost may aid in neutralizing 
it. Add several layers of ash every 18 inches 
(46 cm).
4. V
entilation and oxygen supply – Air,
especially oxygen, is necessary for the
multiplication of microbes involved in the
decomposition of organic materials. Turn
the compost regularly to ensure proper air
circulation, which speeds the decomposition
process by encouraging beneficial bacteria
and fungi growth. Proper ventilation and
oxygen supply can reduce nitrogen loss and
odour production, as well as the moisture
of the compost products, making it easy to
store.
Materials and tools needed for 
compost preparation.
• Plant materials : Fresh leaves and grasses,
brown plant materials, dry grasses, leaves
and crop residues, topsoil, manure or forest
soil, wood charcoal fragments, ashes and
water.
• T
ools: Hand hoes, rakes, spades, forked
hoes, forked spades and buckets.
Note: During composting, avoid using diseased 
plants and materials with a high proportion 
of weed seeds, materials with characteristics 
that are toxic to other plants such as bamboo 
and Eucalyptus, and acidic behaviour upon 
decomposition.

1. Store the compost in sacks in a shaded area.

Preparation of solid organic fertilizers
Farmyard manure
Farmyard manure is a decomposed mixture 
of dung, urine and bedding material (usually 
straw or grass). Manure from different animals 
have different qualities, which thus requires 
different application rates when used as fertilizer 
resource, for instance:
• Cattle, sheep and goat manure are good 
sources of nitrogen and potassium as well 
as organic carbon, while pig manure is 
relatively low in both.
• Chicken manure is highly concentrated in 
nitrogen and phosphate, and is prized for 
both properties.
Characteristics of farmyard manure
• It contains large amounts of nutrients.
• Only part of the nitrogen content of the 
manure is directly available to plants, while 
the remaining part is released as the manure 
decomposes.
• The nitrogen in animal urine is available in 
the short term.
• When dung and urine are mixed, they form 
a well-balanced source of nutrients for 
plants.
• The availability of phosphorus and 
potassium from farmyard manure is similar 
to that from chemical fertilizers.
• Chicken manure is rich in phosphorus. 
However, it is important to be aware of the 
origin of the manure, since chicken manure 
from conventional farms is contaminated 
by heavy metals Heavy metal pollution 
is the main risk during livestock manure 
composting, in which microorganisms play a 
vital role.4

Organic manures contribute to the build-up 
of soil organic matter and thus improve soil 
fertility. 
Collection and storage of farmyard manure
1. Selection of the storage site. The storage 
site should be next to the stables and 
impermeable, and if stored on a heap, 
it should have a slight slope for proper 
drainage of excess water away from the 
manure pile.
2. Collection of farmyard manure. If 
the animals are kept in stables, different 
materials such as bedding and feed 
remains, and other related materials can be 
collected for farmyard manure preparation.
3. Storage of farmyard manure. Manure 
should be stored under anaerobic 
conditions. Water-logged pits result in 
inferior product quality. For storage as a 
heap, the manure should be mixed with dry 
plant material (straw, grass, crop residues, 
leaves, etc.) to absorb the liquid. The straw 
that has been cut or mashed by spreading 
it out on a roadside can absorb more water 
than a long straw.
Note: Poring manure in pits is particularly 
suitable for dry areas and seasons. Storage in 
pits reduces the risk of drying out and the need 
to water the pile. 
Management of the stored farmyard 
manure
The farmyard manure should always be 
protected from sun, rain and waterlogging to 
avoid nutrient losses.
Note: If white fungus appears (threads and 
white spots), the manure is too dry and should 
be dampened with water or urine. A yellow-
green colour and a bad smell are signs that the 
manure is too wet and not sufficiently aerated. 
If the manure shows a brown to black colour 
throughout the heap, the conditions are ideal.
Application of farmyard manure
The method of application depends on the time 
of application and type of the crops. Before 
planting, farmyard manure should be spread 
throughout the farm, depending on the size; 
for example, for a 1m2, 20 kg of manure can 
be applied. For perennial crops, 20 kg can be 
applied per hole, and for cereals, legumes and 
vegetables, 1–2 kg per hole. Farmyard manure 
should be incorporated into the soil before 
planting to enhance nutrient mineralization. 
Green manure preparation
In agriculture, green manure is a crop 
specifically cultivated to be incorporated into the 
soil while still green.
Typically, the green manure’s biomass is 
incorporated during ploughing. The primary 
goal is to add organic matter to the soil for 
its benefit. Green manuring is often used with 
legume crops to add nitrogen to the soil for 
following crops, especially in organic farming, 
but is also used in conventional farming, as 
follows:
• Trees and/or shrubs growing alongside 
crops in an agroforestry system may provide 
a large quantity of green material that can 
be used as green manure.
• Incorporated fresh plant material 
decomposes and releases nutrients quickly.
Selection of plant varieties for green 
manure
Plant varieties that are mostly used as green 
manure include legumes, grasses, wild 
sunflower and moringa leaves.
Preparation and application of green 
manure
• Sow seeds/plants for green manure by 
considering the ideal condition for them to 
germinate, and species to be grown.
• Wait until the biomass is developed to 
provide good nutrients in the soil when

Integrated soil fertility management (ISFM) 
is a holistic approach aimed at nurturing 
soil fertility and agricultural productivity 
sustainably. Recognizing soil fertility as a 
dynamic and multifaceted system, ISFM 
acknowledges the interplay of diverse factors 
such as nutrient dynamics, organic matter 
content and soil structure, and the symbiotic 
relationships between plants, soil organisms and 
microorganisms.
The European Union-funded programme, 
“Capacity Building Related to Multilateral 
Environmental Agreements in African, 
Caribbean and Pacific Countries Phase III” 
(ACP MEAs 3), implemented field activities in 
the United Republic of Tanzania to mainstream 
biodiversity into the agricultural sector. The 
programme underscored the need for enhancing 
the knowledge base of farmers and extension 
service providers in areas such as agroforestry 
systems and ISFM, which led to the development 
of a training manual tailored to local contexts.
The integrated soil fertility management: Training 
guide for facilitators aims to democratize 
knowledge and empower stakeholders at the 
grassroots level. By distilling complex concepts 
into accessible guidance, it equips facilitators, 
farmers, extension officers and development 
agentswith the tools needed to implement ISFM 
approaches effectively.
